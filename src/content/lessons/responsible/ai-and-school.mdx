A chatbot can write a whole essay in ten seconds. So why do you still have to do homework? Because homework is not really about the answer. It is about what happens in your head while you find it.

## The gym and the forklift

Think of homework as a gym. The weights are heavy on purpose, because lifting them makes you stronger. If a forklift lifts them for you, the weights go up and you learn nothing.

AI can be your coach, or it can be the forklift. The trick is to pick the coach.

## Be a study buddy, not an answer machine

Here are ways to use AI that help you learn:

- "Explain photosynthesis in simpler words."
- "Quiz me on this week's spellings, one question at a time."
- "Here is my answer and how I got it. Can you spot a mistake in my thinking?"
- "Give me a hint, not the answer."

And here is the one that does not: "Write my report." That is the forklift. You hand in work you did not do, and you skip the learning. Quizzing yourself is a good example of what to do instead, since trying to remember something is one of the best ways to learn it.

## Check the rules first

Schools do not agree about AI. Some allow it for studying, some for drafting, and some not at all. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that four out of five US high-school and college students now use AI for schoolwork. It also says only about half of middle and high schools have AI policies, and just 6% of teachers say those policies are clear. That was in the US, but the lesson works anywhere: do not guess.

So ask your teacher. "May I use AI for this task, and for what?" It takes ten seconds.

## Use your own words

Write first, then ask for feedback. If you paste in AI's sentences, they are not your thoughts. Read what it says, close it, and explain it in your own words. If you cannot, you have not learned it yet.

## Say when you used it

If your school asks you to say when you used AI, say so. A short note is enough: "I used AI to quiz me and check my spelling. I wrote the answers myself." Being open is always safer than keeping quiet.

## Check the facts

You have seen that a chatbot writes what sounds right, which is why it can make things up. You can read about that in [How a chatbot answers](../../start/how-a-chatbot-answers/). So check names, numbers, dates and quotes in your textbook or on a trusted website. A chatbot that says "yes, that is correct" has not checked anything.

## Ask AI well

Good answers start with good questions. Four things help:

1. **A clear task.** Say what you want, not just a topic.
2. **Who it is for.** Your age, or what you already know.
3. **An example or a shape.** "Five short sentences" or "compare it to something from home".
4. **Steps, not just answers.** Ask it to check you, or to go one step at a time.

<Deep title="For teachers and parents">

A short class rule helps: say which tasks AI may be used for, whether students must note their use, and what to do if unsure. Assessments that show thinking, such as drafts, a quick spoken check or work done in class, make it less tempting to hand in work that is not the student's own.
