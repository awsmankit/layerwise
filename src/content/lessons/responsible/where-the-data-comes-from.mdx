Two questions come up most: "Is it copying?" and "Can I use what it makes, or what I build on?" Neither has one answer, because they are several questions about different things.

## Where training data comes from

| Source | What it is | The question it raises |
|---|---|---|
| Web crawl | Pages fetched by crawlers. Common Crawl publishes one, and its crawler checks `robots.txt` first | Having a copy is not having a licence. Common Crawl's terms say you agree to respect third-party copyrights and "strongly" recommend legal advice before use, including commercial use |
| Licensed data | Books, news, images or code supplied under a deal or an open licence | What does the licence let you do with a trained model? |
| User data | Prompts, feedback and files from customers | Consent, purpose and memorisation: see [privacy engineering for AI](../privacy-engineering-for-ai/) |
| Synthetic data | Text or images written by another model | The data inherits the original's licence and the provider's terms (below) |
| Shadow libraries | Collections of pirated books | The one source courts have treated differently (below) |

## Three questions, not one

Copyright gives owners exclusive rights, including copying. The US Copyright Office, in a pre-publication report (its hub page still promised a final version on 1 November 2026), concluded that several stages of building a generative model touch those rights, so the key question is whether fair use excuses them. That "will depend on what works were used, from what source, for what purpose, and with what controls on the outputs". Commercial use of vast troves of work to produce competing expressive content, "especially where this is accomplished through illegal access", goes beyond established fair use boundaries. On weights, infringement turns on whether the model "has retained or memorized substantial protectable expression".

So keep three questions apart:

1. **The input:** is copying works into a dataset and training on them allowed?
2. **The model:** do the weights contain the works?
3. **The output:** can the model reproduce them, and who owns what it makes?

## What courts and regulators have said so far

These are dated snapshots from the primary documents. Each decided its own facts under its own country's law. None of them settles the question.

**United States** (fair use, decided case by case):

- **Bartz v. Anthropic**, 23 June 2025. The judge held that training on the books was "exceedingly transformative" and a fair use, and that scanning purchased print books was also fair. He refused to excuse building a permanent library from pirated copies, and set that for trial. A class settlement followed, and a different judge granted final approval on 20 July 2026: $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per work, releasing past copying only, not AI outputs or future conduct. A settlement is not a ruling on the law.
- **Kadrey v. Meta**, 25 June 2025. A different judge in the same court granted Meta summary judgment on thirteen authors' training claim, but wrote that the ruling "does not stand for the proposition that Meta's use of copyrighted materials to train its language models is lawful": the authors had not built a record on market dilution, which he called the potentially winning argument.
- **Thomson Reuters v. ROSS**, Third Circuit, 29 September 2026. The appeals court held that copying Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal search tool was not fair use. The panel stressed that ROSS's tool was not generative, so the generative cases were different. It is the only appeals decision here.
- **In re OpenAI** (S.D.N.Y.) is pending. I could not read its docket.

**European Union:**

- **The rules.** AI Act recital 105 explains that EU law has text-and-data-mining exceptions, that rights holders may reserve their rights ("opt out") except for scientific research, and that where they did so in an appropriate manner, providers need authorisation. Article 53(1) requires providers of general-purpose AI models to have a copyright policy that identifies and respects such reservations, and to publish a training-content summary using the Commission's mandatory template (from 2 August 2025, or 2 August 2027 for models already on the market).
- **Kneschke v LAION**, Hamburg appeal court, 10 December 2025. The court held that building the dataset was covered by the text-and-data-mining exception, because the image agency's reservation on its website did not have the machine-readable form the law requires, and also by the scientific-research exception. It allowed a further appeal to the Federal Court of Justice. I did not find the outcome.
- **GEMA v OpenAI**, Munich Regional Court, 11 November 2025. The court held that song lyrics reproducible from the models were embodied in their parameters, that this was reproduction, and that the text-and-data-mining exception, which it said covers preparatory copying, did not cover copies inside the model. It held the defendants responsible for the outputs. It is not final.

**United Kingdom:**

- **Getty Images v Stability AI**, 4 November 2025. Getty dropped its claim that training happened in the UK, and its claim about outputs. On what remained, the judge held that Stable Diffusion's weights "do not store" copies of the works and "have never contained or stored an infringing copy", so the model was not an "infringing copy" under the UK statute. Notice the difference from Munich: they are different laws and different evidence, and they disagree on whether weights can count as a copy.
- **Government report**, March 2026. A broad exception with an opt-out is "no longer" the preferred way forward. There will be no reform until the government is confident, but more evidence-gathering and work on transparency and licensing. The existing exception covers non-commercial research only.

<Deep title="Why the same facts can go different ways">
US fair use weighs purpose, the work, the amount and market effect, per case. EU and UK law use listed exceptions with conditions and ask whether a particular act of copying is covered. A US judge can call training transformative, a German court can put a memorised copy outside a mining exception, and an English court can find no copy in the weights, without contradicting each other. Where the training happened matters too: Getty dropped its UK training claim for lack of evidence it happened in the UK.
</Deep>

## robots.txt and opt-outs

`robots.txt` is a file saying which paths crawlers should avoid. The standard, RFC 9309, says the rules are what crawlers "are requested to honor" and "are not a form of access authorization". It is a polite request, and a binding one only where a law or a contract makes it so. In the EU, the AI Act and the Hamburg ruling show why the form of an opt-out matters: it has to be expressed "in an appropriate manner", and the Hamburg court found that the agency's reservation did not have the machine-readable form the law requires. An IETF working group, AI Preferences, is chartered to standardise a vocabulary for expressing such preferences. As of 1 November 2026 I did not review any finished standard.

In practice: if you publish, state your wishes in several machine-readable ways and in your terms. If you crawl, honour `robots.txt`, keep a log of what you fetched and when, and keep any opt-out signals you respected.

## Three licences, not one

A model has three layers, and each has its own licence: the **code** that trains and runs it, the **weights**, and the **data**. People often check one and forget the others.

- **Data licences.** Creative Commons combines Attribution with NonCommercial, ShareAlike or NoDerivatives, and CC0 is a waiver. CC's FAQ says that if a use needs no permission, because an exception applies, you need not comply with the licence, and that commercial mining should avoid NC material. ODbL has share-alike for derivative databases. CDLA-Permissive-2.0 says "Results" of computational analysis, "including for example machine learning models", carry no restriction. None of these texts says whether a trained model is an "adaptation" or "derivative database" of its data.
- **Model licences.** Apache 2.0 and MIT are permissive with notice duties. The Llama 3.3 licence, a worked example of a custom one, adds an acceptable-use policy, "Built with Llama" and naming rules, and a licence request above 700 million monthly users. The BigScience RAIL licence passes its use restrictions on to users and counts training on the model's outputs as a derivative.
- **What "open" means.** The Open Source Initiative's definition of open-source AI asks for the parameters, the code and detailed "data information", which many "open-weights" models do not provide. See [choosing and comparing models](../../llm/choosing-and-comparing-models/) and [private and air-gapped AI](../../local/private-and-air-gapped-ai/).
- **Outputs and provider terms.** The Anthropic Commercial Terms (section D.4) and the Gemini API terms, as read on 1 November 2026, both bar using the service to build competing models. OpenAI's terms blocked automated reading, so I have not read them. Check the current terms for your plan before you train on any provider's outputs.
- **Who owns what it makes.** The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that outputs can be copyrightable in whole or part where AI is a tool and a human determines the expressive elements, and that prompts alone are unlikely to be enough.

## Memorisation

Memorisation is where the three questions meet: the Copyright Office ties the weights question to it, and the Munich and London courts reached opposite findings about what the weights hold. [Privacy engineering for AI](../privacy-engineering-for-ai/) covers how memorisation is measured and reduced.

## A provenance checklist

- [ ] Every dataset has a source, a licence or other basis, a date fetched and a person responsible, recorded in one place.
- [ ] Licences are read for the exact version: dataset, weights and code.
- [ ] `robots.txt`, site terms and machine-readable opt-outs were respected and logged.
- [ ] Personal data follows your privacy process.
- [ ] Synthetic data records which model made it, under which terms.
- [ ] You can answer "was this work in the training set?" for a model version.
- [ ] If you provide a general-purpose model in the EU, the copyright policy and training summary exist.
- [ ] A named person owns the legal questions, and has seen the open ones.

## What to remember

- "Is it legal?" is three questions: the input, the model, the output. Keep them apart.
- As of 1 November 2026 there is no settled answer on AI training and copyright. Courts in the US, Germany and the UK have decided narrow cases, differently, and some are on appeal.
- A source being easy to download does not make it licensed. Where the data came from, and whether you respected opt-outs, matters in the cases above.
- A model, its code and its data carry separate licences, and licences do not say whether a trained model is an adaptation of its data.
- Keep provenance records now. They are cheap, and they are what you will be asked for later.
