"Will AI take my job?" assumes a job is one thing that a machine either can or cannot do. The evidence is more useful than that question, and less dramatic.

## Jobs are bundles of tasks

Economists model work as **tasks**, the units a job is made of. Acemoglu and Autor's task-based model lets technology replace labour in some tasks while others stay with people, and Autor's 2015 essay argues that automation both substitutes for labour and complements it. Whether a job shrinks, changes or grows depends on which tasks are affected, how much time they take, and what happens to demand for the finished work.

Eloundou and colleagues rated US occupations by whether a large language model could cut the time a task takes. Combining human and GPT-4 ratings, they found around 80% of US workers could have at least 10% of their tasks affected, and about 19% could have at least half. The authors say they make no prediction about timing or adoption. That is an **exposure** measure: it says a tool could touch the task, not that an employer will use it, that it will be good enough, or that jobs will go. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) states this directly: "Exposure does not imply job loss, productivity gains, automation probability, or wage effects."

## What field studies measured

Five well-known studies, each read at the level of its own abstract or page. None of them measures jobs lost.

| Study | Setting | Result reported | What it leaves out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (QJE, 2025) | 5,172 customer-support agents at one firm; staggered rollout of an AI assistant | Issues resolved per hour up 15% on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved speed and quality; the most experienced saw small speed gains and small quality declines | One firm, one tool, one kind of work |
| Noy and Zhang (Science, 2023) | 453 college-educated professionals in a preregistered online experiment, midlevel writing tasks | Time down 40%, quality up 18%; inequality between workers fell | Short, incentivised tasks, not a whole job over months |
| Peng and others (2023) | Recruited developers asked to write an HTTP server in JavaScript | The group with Copilot finished 55.8% faster | One small, well-defined task |
| Dell'Acqua and others (HBS working paper, 2023) | 758 BCG consultants, GPT-4, preregistered | On 18 tasks inside the AI's range: 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, higher quality. On one task outside it, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to be correct | One firm, one model (GPT-4, 2023); the outside-range task was chosen to be hard for AI |
| METR (2025) | 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks in their own projects, each randomly allowed or not allowed AI | Tasks took 19% longer with AI. Developers had expected 24% faster, and afterwards believed it was 20% faster | By the authors' own caveats: not representative of most software work, only software, a snapshot of early-2025 tools |

The numbers look contradictory (55.8% faster, 19% slower) until you compare what was measured: different tasks, people, tools and dates. The METR result also moved. METR now says on its page that the 2025 results are out of date. Its February 2026 follow-up (57 developers, 800+ tasks) gave estimates of -18% for returning developers and -4% for new ones, meaning shorter task times, but both intervals include zero, and METR calls the data "an unreliable signal of the current productivity effect of AI tools". Many developers would not work without AI, and between 30% and 50% held back tasks they would not do without it. So the honest summary is not "AI slows developers" or "AI speeds them up". It is that a careful lab could not get a clean answer twice, for good reasons.

One finding from the 2025 trial is about perception, not speed: developers believed AI had sped them up by about 20% when the measured effect was the opposite. Time yourself. For how coding tools are used well, see [AI-assisted software development](../../llm/ai-assisted-software-development/).

## What labour statistics show

Official statistics and early payroll studies measure real employment, which the experiments cannot.

- **BLS projections (published 27 August 2026).** Total employment is projected to grow 3.5%, from 170.3 million to 176.2 million, between 2025 and 2035. Office and administrative support is projected to fall 4.0% (752,100 jobs, the most of any major group), which BLS links mainly to "the continued integration of automation tools, including those powered by AI". Sales and related occupations are projected to fall 1.4%, partly from AI tools in the sales process. Data scientists are projected to grow 34.6%. BLS says AI's effects are highly uncertain: "the uncertainty about potential impacts remains very high". These are projections under stated assumptions, not forecasts, and BLS applies AI adjustments "conservatively".
- **Payroll data, United States.** Stanford's Digital Economy Lab (revision of August 2026, using ADP payroll data) reports no "widespread, economy-wide job displacement associated with AI", but employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most exposed occupations about 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers (15% in the July 2025 data). Experienced workers show no comparable gap. The authors write that these are "descriptive patterns, not causal estimates", that gaps shrink when education is accounted for, and that some trends predate generative AI.
- **Administrative data, Denmark.** Humlum and Vestergaard (revised March 2026) combine surveys and official records and find precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects above 2% two years after ChatGPT launched. They find that work was reorganised: employers reassigned tasks around content generation and AI oversight.

These do not conflict as much as they seem. They cover different groups (all workers or young workers in exposed jobs), different outcomes (earnings and hours, or headcount and hiring), different countries and different dates. They are early and descriptive.

## Who seems to gain

Several studies find the biggest gains for the least experienced: support agents, writers and, in the BCG study, below-average consultants. That points to AI narrowing gaps between people doing the same task. Autor's 2024 paper argues, as a possibility and not a forecast, that AI could extend expert judgement to more workers.

The payroll evidence points to a worry on a different axis: fewer entry-level hires in exposed occupations, which could matter for how people gain experience. Neither finding cancels the other. At the level of the whole economy, Acemoglu's task-based estimate (2024, under the paper's stated assumptions) is for a total productivity gain of no more than 0.66% over ten years, and he argues the gap between capital and labour income may widen. Other economists use other assumptions. Treat every economy-wide number as a scenario, not a result.

## Skills and training

The evidence on training is thin. The most useful finding is the BCG one: consultants who used AI on a task outside its range did worse, and the paper says they relied too much on it, so judgement about where AI helps matters. A 2025 study asked 1,500 workers which of 844 tasks they would want AI agents to automate or assist, and compared that with experts' view of what the technology can do. It suggests mismatches in both directions, plus early signs of skill demands shifting from information handling towards interpersonal skills. It is stated preferences plus expert opinion, not observed outcomes. The International Labour Organization's 2025 update, which finds about one in four workers in an occupation with some exposure, concludes that most such jobs are more likely transformed than eliminated, and calls for managing the change through social dialogue.

## What is unknown

- How fast tools improve, and how fast employers change how work is organised.
- Whether results from one firm or one task type carry over. Most studies cover knowledge work in rich countries.
- Effects on pay beyond the first two years, and on how people build skills early in a career.
- Which new tasks appear. Statistics built on today's job lists cannot see them.
- Whether benefits are shared. That depends on policy, bargaining and market power as much as technology.

## Planning without a forecast

**For workers.** List your tasks with rough hours, as in the sorter. Try tools on low-stakes ones and time yourself. Keep up the habit of checking, because the evidence says over-trust costs people. Ask what training and rules your employer has. Build knowledge of your own field, which is what lets you tell good output from plausible output.

**For managers.** Pilot with a comparison group and measure quality and rework, not just speed. Do not carry a laboratory number over to your own work. Decide in advance what happens to freed time. Use [human oversight](../human-oversight-and-when-ai-should-not-decide/) that really works, and involve staff early. Check legal duties with a qualified adviser, since this lesson gives no legal advice; see [the EU AI Act for engineers](../../evals/eu-ai-act-for-engineers/) and [running AI governance](../running-ai-governance/).

<Deep title="Why one number is never enough: effect, population and measure">

A productivity study answers a narrow question: for these people, on these tasks, with this tool, over this period, what changed in the measured outcome? To get from there to "this job" you need to know the share of the job's time spent on such tasks and how quality, rework and demand respond. To get to "this economy" you also need how many firms adopt, how prices move and how labour markets adjust. Every step adds assumptions.

Randomised trials give strong internal validity for the setting studied. Payroll and administrative data cover real employment but cannot easily separate AI from interest rates, post-pandemic hiring patterns or education effects, which is why the Stanford authors call their results descriptive. The METR follow-up shows another hazard: if people who benefit most refuse to take part, the estimate shifts. Look for the population, the outcome, the comparison and the date before you trust a headline.

</Deep>

## What to remember

- Jobs are bundles of tasks. Exposure of a task is not job loss.
- Field studies measure tasks and productivity, not jobs lost, and they differ in tasks, people, tools and dates.
- The same lab can get two different answers a year apart. Check the date and what the authors themselves say about their limits.
- Official projections and payroll data show no broad collapse in employment so far, along with early signs of weaker hiring for young workers in exposed jobs. Both are descriptive and uncertain.
- Gains appear largest for less experienced workers in several studies, and the evidence on careers and entry-level hiring is still open.
- Plan by measuring your own tasks, keeping checking skills sharp and asking what happens to freed time.
