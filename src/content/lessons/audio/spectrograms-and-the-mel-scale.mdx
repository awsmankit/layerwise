[How a computer hears](../how-a-computer-hears/) ended with a long list of samples: 16,000 numbers a second for speech. That list is faithful, but it is a poor thing to hand to a model. This lesson covers what most speech models read instead, and why.

## Why not read the raw waveform?

A waveform shows how loud the air pressure is at each instant. What makes an "ah" different from an "ee" is something else: which pitches are present in the sound, and how strong each one is. That is hard to see in a wavy line, and a model would have to work it out from scratch.

The numbers are also fragile. Shift a sound a hair earlier or later and almost every sample changes, although it still sounds the same. A model reading samples has to learn to ignore that.

Some models do read raw audio: wav2vec 2.0 feeds the waveform into a stack of convolutions. But many speech models, Whisper (a speech-to-text model, also called ASR, automatic speech recognition) among them, are given a time-frequency picture. Getting that picture takes three ideas.

## Idea 1: a sound is a sum of pure tones

A **pure tone** is the smoothest possible wave, a sine wave, with one pitch. Real sounds are mixtures. A voice, a violin and a clap are each a sum of many pure tones at different pitches and strengths.

The **Fourier transform** is the calculation that works backwards. Give it a stretch of sound and it tells you which pure tones are inside and how strong each one is. The result is a **spectrum**: strength for each frequency. The fast way to compute it is called the FFT.

Mix some tones below and watch the bars. The wave on top is built by adding the tones. The bars underneath were measured from that wave alone by a real transform, and they find the ingredients you mixed.

## Idea 2: short windows make a spectrogram

One transform of a whole sentence says which pitches occurred, but not when. Speech changes every few hundredths of a second. So we cut the sound into short pieces called **windows**, transform each one, and line the results up side by side.

The picture that results is a **spectrogram**. Time runs across, frequency runs up, and brightness shows energy. Each column is one window's spectrum.

Windows overlap: the **hop** is how far we slide forward each time. Whisper uses a 25 ms window and a 10 ms hop. At 16,000 samples a second, that is a 400-sample window sliding 160 samples at a time, giving 100 columns for every second of audio. Each window is also faded in and out at its edges (with a Hann window) so the sudden cut does not smear the result.

## Idea 3: time versus frequency detail

A window of N samples at rate R gives frequency detail of R divided by N. A long window sees pitch sharply but blurs timing. A short window pinpoints moments but blurs pitch. You cannot have both. These figures are computed for a 16,000 Hz rate:

| Window | Samples | Frequency detail | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.25 ms | 100 | 160 Hz | Sharp moments, such as a click |
| 25 ms | 400 | 40 Hz | A balance, used for speech |
| 100 ms | 1,600 | 10 Hz | Steady tones, such as a held note |

A voice buzzing at 120 Hz also has energy at 240, 360, 480 Hz and so on. These whole-number multiples are called **harmonics**, and they are 120 Hz apart. A 160 Hz resolution cannot separate them. A 40 Hz resolution can.

## Hearing is not evenly spaced

Look at the plain view of the vowel. Most of the bright detail sits in the lower part of the picture, and the upper half is faint.

Our ears work this way. The step from 100 Hz to 200 Hz sounds large: it is an octave. The step from 5,000 Hz to 5,100 Hz is barely noticeable, although both are 100 Hz. The **mel scale** is a pitch scale built from listening tests in which people judged which pitches were equally far apart. In the original definition, a 1,000 Hz tone is 1,000 mel. One common formula is mel = 2,595 x log10(1 + f / 700).

By that formula, 100 Hz to 200 Hz is about 133 mel, and 5,000 Hz to 5,100 Hz is only about 20 mel, around 7 times smaller. (Whisper's filters come from librosa's default, a Slaney-style scale that is straight below 1 kHz and logarithmic above it. The numbers differ a little; the shape is the same.)

## Mel bands: squeezing the picture

A **mel filterbank** applies this idea to a spectrum. It adds up the energy into overlapping triangular bands whose centres are evenly spaced in mel. That gives many narrow bands at low pitches and a few wide ones high up. Whisper uses 80 bands between 0 and 8,000 Hz: the lowest is about 74 Hz wide and the highest about 590 Hz wide, around 8 times wider (computed from the Slaney scale).

<MelBands client:visible />

A 400-sample window gives 201 frequency bins (up to 8,000 Hz in 40 Hz steps). The filterbank turns each column of 201 numbers into 80. The result is a **mel spectrogram**.

## Log scaling

Loudness is not linear for ears either. Energy in a spectrogram can vary by a factor of billions, from a whisper to a shout. So the energies are converted to a logarithmic scale. Whisper takes log10, stops values falling more than 8 below the loudest value in the clip (a factor of 10^8 in power), then shifts and scales them into a range a network handles well (the paper says roughly -1 to 1). The final picture is a **log-mel spectrogram**.

So the model sees an image-like grid, 80 tall, and reads it as a sequence of frames, one every 10 ms, a lot like a sentence of tokens.

<Callout kind="edge" title="Frames set the cost">
A frame every 10 ms means 100 frames a second, so cost grows with the hop. A 25 ms window also means a system must hear 25 ms of sound before it can make the first column. That is already longer than the 5 to 6 ms delays rated just acceptable in the hearing-aid study quoted in the previous lesson, so a product that must respond that fast has to use much shorter frames or other methods. <Deep title="Worked numbers and a Python sketch">

**One bin.** A 1,000 Hz tone at 16,000 samples a second, cut into a 400-sample Hann window, has a transform peak exactly in bin 25 (25 x 40 Hz = 1,000 Hz). A 1,030 Hz tone falls between bins 25 and 26 and spreads over both. This site's tests check those values against numpy and librosa.

**Sketch.** This is close to, but not identical to, Whisper's own code:

```python
import librosa, numpy as np
y, sr = librosa.load("clip.wav", sr=16000)            # resample to 16 kHz
mel = librosa.feature.melspectrogram(
    y=y, sr=sr, n_fft=400, hop_length=160, n_mels=80)  # (80, frames)
log_mel = np.log10(np.maximum(mel, 1e-10))
log_mel = np.maximum(log_mel, log_mel.max() - 8.0)     # keep within 8 of the loudest
log_mel = (log_mel + 4.0) / 4.0
```

For one second of audio this gives 101 columns. Whisper's own code drops the final one, and uses magnitudes squared, as librosa does.

**MFCCs.** A classical, more compact cousin takes the discrete cosine transform of the log-mel values and keeps only the first few coefficients. Neural models mostly read the log-mel values directly.

**What the spectrogram throws away.** It keeps strength but not the exact timing of waves inside each window (the phase). That is fine for recognising speech. It means you cannot turn a spectrogram back into sound exactly, so models that produce speech usually need a vocoder to fill that in.

**Many other models use it.** Wake-word detectors, noise suppressors and hearing-aid models also read time-frequency pictures, often with other band layouts. This lesson covers the shared idea; later lessons build on it.

</Deep>

## What to remember

- A sound is a sum of pure tones. The Fourier transform finds which ones and how strong.
- A spectrogram applies it to short, overlapping windows: time across, frequency up, brightness for energy.
- Long windows give sharp pitch and blurred timing. Short windows give the reverse. Whisper's 25 ms window with a 10 ms hop is a speech compromise.
- The mel scale spaces bands the way hearing does, with fine detail low and coarse detail high. Taking logs mirrors how loudness is heard.
- Whisper reads an 80 by 3,000 log-mel grid for each 30 seconds of 16 kHz audio (128 bands in large-v3).
