[Speech recognition](../speech-recognition/) ended with models that can invent words from silence or noise. People with hearing loss meet the noise problem every day, with no transcript to fall back on. The US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders says a hearing aid has three basic parts, a microphone, an amplifier and a speaker, and "does not completely separate the sounds you want to hear from the ones you do not want to hear". This lesson is about the part of the chain that tries.

## The task, and a number for it

You have a noisy mixture and want the speech. How noisy it is gets measured as the **signal-to-noise ratio** (SNR): 10 times the base-10 logarithm of speech energy over noise energy, in decibels (dB). At 0 dB they are equal. At 10 dB speech has ten times the energy. At 5 dB, where the lab below starts, about 3.2 times.

A device has two kinds of tool. **Spatial** processing uses several microphones to favour one direction. **Spectral** processing works on one signal and decides, for each moment and frequency, how much to turn it down. Products combine both.

## Directional microphones in one picture

Put two omnidirectional microphones a few millimetres apart. Delay the rear signal a little and subtract it from the front one, and sound from some directions cancels. The delay sets the pattern. When it equals the time sound takes to cross the gap, the rear is cancelled completely: a **cardioid**. The audiology literature calls this a first-order subtractive directional microphone (Jespersen, 2023).

<BeamPolar client:visible />

Worked numbers for a 10 mm gap, from the plane-wave formula behind the figure (examples, not a product): sound crosses the gap in about 29 microseconds, under half a sample period at 16 kHz. The cardioid is about 23 dB quieter straight ahead than one microphone at 200 Hz, 9 dB quieter at 1 kHz, and 2.5 dB louder at 4 kHz.

1. **The cost is bass.** Subtracting nearly identical signals leaves little; the loss grows 6 dB per octave downwards. Designs add low-frequency gain back, which also amplifies the microphones' own noise (Jespersen, 2023).
2. **Spacing is a compromise.** The same chapter reports 5 to 12 mm as appropriate and commonly used in hearing aids.
3. **It assumes the talker is in front.** It favours a direction; it does not know who is talking. Bilateral beamformers combine the four microphones of two aids, and adaptive versions that change pattern with the noise have reached commercial products (J Am Acad Audiol, 2020). In a laboratory study with 18 adults, directional microphones improved sentence recognition and listening effort but not localisation (Int J Audiol, 2018).

## Spectral subtraction and Wiener filtering

One microphone can still help, because speech and noise look different in a spectrogram. Valin (2018) describes the structure common to most noise suppressors: a voice activity detector says when only noise is present, a noise estimator learns its spectrum, and a spectral estimator turns each cell down.

In **spectral subtraction** a cell's gain is

`gain = max(floor, 1 - alpha x noise / signal)`

where *noise* is the estimated noise level at that frequency, *signal* the level in this cell, *alpha* above 1 over-subtracts, and the *floor* stops the gain reaching zero (as in the 2023 paper listed below). A **Wiener filter** uses the estimated cell SNR instead: gain = SNR / (1 + SNR).

They share a weakness. Noise in any one frame is not smooth; its level in each cell jumps about. Where it exceeds the estimate a cell survives, where it falls below it is cut. What remains is a scatter of isolated spots that change every frame, heard as short chirping tones: **musical noise**. The standard cure is the floor, which leaves some broadband noise to mask the spots. Subtracting harder is no cure: the same paper notes that as alpha rises the broadband noise falls but the spots stand out more.

3. **Spectral subtraction on hiss.** Set alpha 1 and the floor 60 dB: scattered dots in the silent lead-in, tens of blips per thousand cells. Set the floor to 6 dB and they vanish under the leftover noise. Alpha 3 also lowers the count, but only because nearly every cell is cut: look at "Speech kept".
4. **Babble.** Subtraction and Wiener gain much less, because the estimate from the first 0.45 s does not describe the babble that follows. The oracle mask, which sees the answer, still does well.
5. **Read more than the headline.** The harshest subtraction often has the best SNR improvement and the worst "Speech kept".

At 5 dB SNR, subtraction (alpha 2, 20 dB floor) improved steady hiss by about 13 dB and babble by about 3 dB; the oracle improved babble by about 9 dB. These are examples from this synthetic clip, not claims about real speech or any product.

## Neural speech enhancement

A trained network replaces the hand-built estimators. Commonly it learns a **mask**: a number from 0 to 1 for each time-frequency cell. Wang and Chen's overview defines the targets. The ideal binary mask keeps a cell if speech beats noise by a margin. The **ideal ratio mask** (IRM) is the soft version: (speech energy / (speech + noise energy)) raised to a power, usually 0.5. The oracle in the lab is exactly that. Other models predict complex masks that fix phase, apply short filters across frames (DeepFilterNet), or work on the waveform.

Training data is made by mixing clean speech and noise at chosen SNRs, so the right answer is known. RNNoise, a small open model, made 140 hours of noisy speech from 6 hours of speech and 4 hours of noise by varying gains and filters. It combines classical processing with a recurrent network that estimates gains for 22 bands. Its paper reports 87,503 weights and about 40 Mflops (millions of floating-point operations a second) in real time, about 14 percent of a 1.2 GHz Arm Cortex-A53 core (a Raspberry Pi 3).

Waveform models skip the long spectrogram window: Conv-TasNet's authors note that spectrogram-based systems typically use windows of at least 32 ms, while their best configuration uses learned 2 ms filters.

## The delay budget

A transform-based processor cannot finish a frame until its whole window has arrived, so the window length is roughly a floor on delay. Listening tests say how much people tolerate, and it depends on the situation. A wearer hears the aid's output mixed with the same sound arriving directly, so delay becomes an echo. In simulations of open-canal fittings, a disturbance rating of 3 ("just acceptable") came at about 5 to 6 ms (Stone et al., 2008). 3 to 5 ms was noticeable in 76 percent of trials and over 10 ms objectionable in 90 percent for people hearing their own voice (Agnew and Thornton, 2000). Delays of 20 to 30 ms were "disturbing" for mild to moderate simulated losses (Stone and Moore, 1999). Tolerance was greater for listeners with hearing loss in two of three conditions, in a set-up that differed from commercial aids (Goehring et al., 2019).

A 2023 Clarity challenge entry shows one way to cope: an asymmetric window pair keeps a 16 ms analysis window but cuts algorithmic latency to 4 ms, to meet a 5 ms limit.

## How hearing devices combine these ideas

What follows is what manufacturers and researchers have published, as of 1 November 2026. It is not a full account of any product, and products change by generation.

- **Order of work.** The Phonak paper (June 2024) describes scene classification, microphone settings, spatial pre-processing, then the neural network for its "Speech in Loud Noise" path, then the usual gain stage. Default microphone mode is fixed directional.
- **The network works on a mask.** In that paper it takes a 64-frequency complex spectrum, has 4.5 million parameters, was trained on 22 million sound samples and outputs a 64-frequency mask. It runs on a separate neural-network chip at 50 MHz, doing 7,700 million operations a second. The Oticon paper (2024) describes a network whose input and output layers each have 24 neurons, matching 24 processing channels, trained with error analysis in 256 channels.
- **On the device.** The Starkey study (October 2025; authors include Stanford researchers and Starkey employees) states that its algorithm runs on the aid's own chip, which has a hardware accelerator for neural networks, without a phone or cloud. In research, Diehl et al. (2023) ran a denoiser in real time on a laptop and suggested hearing-aid chips could follow.
- **Not in these papers.** I found no end-to-end delay or power figure in any of the three, and each reports decibels measured with a different set-up, so do not subtract one company's numbers from another's.
- **The floor, on purpose.** A 2024 study of an "ideal compressed mask" found that limiting the maximum attenuation improved intelligibility while keeping recognition of environmental sounds high. That is the lab's floor slider.

Hearing aids are regulated medical devices. This lesson covers engineering ideas only, not regulatory or clinical advice.

## How enhancement is judged

- **SNR improvement** is cheap and needs the clean signal, but it rewards over-cutting. Pair it with a damage measure such as scale-invariant SDR (Le Roux et al., 2018).
- **Hearing-specific metrics.** The Clarity challenges use HASPI (intelligibility) and HASQI (quality) plus listening tests with hearing-impaired listeners. HASQI was added because some entries raised intelligibility at the cost of naturalness.
- **Listening tests.** The Deep Noise Suppression Challenge organisers argue most conventional objective metrics correlate poorly with subjective tests, and judged winners on crowdsourced ratings (ITU-T P.808).

## What to remember

- SNR is an energy ratio in dB: 0 dB is equal, +10 dB is ten times.
- A directional microphone is two microphones, a delay and a subtraction. It favours the front, costs bass and assumes the talker is there.
- Spectral subtraction and Wiener filtering turn down cells that look like estimated noise. Isolated survivors are musical noise; a floor masks them, and subtracting harder trades them for speech damage.
- Neural enhancement mostly learns a mask or filter from mixtures made at chosen SNRs. Real-time use caps model size and window length.
- Hearing devices live under a delay of a few milliseconds, so long windows need special designs.
- Published manufacturer papers describe mask-based networks on dedicated hardware, not full latency or power. Judge enhancement with several measures and with listeners.
