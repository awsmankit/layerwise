This lesson is about the next step, turning that grid into words, and about the choices that decide whether a speech recogniser is fast, accurate, private or all three.

## The alignment problem

Speech recognition (ASR, for automatic speech recognition) looks like translation: sound in, text out. It is harder in one respect. Audio and text are different lengths and run at different speeds. One second of speech is 100 spectrogram columns but maybe 15 letters, and which letter belongs to which column changes with every speaker.

Nobody labels that by hand. Training data is a recording plus a transcript, with no marks saying "the t starts here". Every ASR design is an answer to one question: how do you learn, and then decode, without knowing the alignment?

## The old pipeline, in one paragraph

For decades the standard answer was a pipeline of separate parts, as taught in textbooks such as [Jurafsky and Martin](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/16.pdf) and implemented in toolkits like [Kaldi](https://kaldi-asr.org/doc/about.html). An **acoustic model** scored how well short slices of audio matched speech sounds. A **pronunciation lexicon** mapped words to sounds. A **language model** scored which word sequences are likely. A **decoder** searched for the word string that best satisfied all three. Each part was trained separately. Modern systems fold most of this into one network.

## Three ways to go end to end

End-to-end models read the features and write characters or word pieces directly. They differ in how they handle the alignment.

| Family | How it handles alignment | Streams easily? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CTC** | One output per audio frame, with a special "blank"; repeats and blanks are collapsed afterwards | Yes | The Connectionist Temporal Classification loss (Graves et al., 2006); wav2vec 2.0 fine-tuned with CTC |
| **Attention encoder-decoder** | An encoder summarises the audio; a decoder writes text one token at a time, looking back at the encoder (attention) | Not by default: it wants the whole segment first | Listen, Attend and Spell; Whisper |
| **Transducer (RNN-T)** | Like CTC, but a small text-side network also looks at what has already been written | Yes | The RNN transducer (Graves, 2012); on-device recognisers such as the one in He et al., 2018 |

Jurafsky and Martin's rule of thumb: CTC models stream well but, because each frame is decided independently of the previous output, tend to be a little less accurate than attention encoder-decoders; transducers add that text-side memory.

wav2vec 2.0 is a training recipe, not a fourth design: it learns from unlabelled audio first, then is fine-tuned on a little transcribed speech. Its authors report 4.8 and 8.2 percent word error on two LibriSpeech test sets (clean and harder) with just ten minutes of labelled data and 53,000 hours of unlabelled audio.

## CTC: the trick that skips alignment

CTC lets a model say something for every frame, and then removes the redundancy with two rules. **Merge** neighbouring repeats, then **drop** the blanks. The blank symbol (written `_` below) means "no new letter in this frame". It is also how a double letter survives: `l l` merges into one l, but `l _ l` becomes two.

Many different frame-by-frame paths collapse to the same text. For a clear model saying "cat" over 10 frames there are 1,716 of them, and the text's probability is the **sum** over all those paths. Training maximises that sum, which is why no one has to supply an alignment (Graves et al., 2006; Hannun, 2017 has a clear visual guide).

4. In the flickering "cat", greedy spells "caat" (about 37 percent) but "cat" is more probable (about 41 percent). A beam of 4 finds it.

These numbers belong to the invented examples, not to a real model.

## Decoding: greedy and beam search

**Greedy decoding** takes the best symbol in each frame and collapses. It is fast and often good enough when the model is confident. Its flaw is the one you just saw: it picks the best *path*, not the best *text*.

**Beam search** keeps several candidate texts alive. For CTC, a prefix beam search stores texts rather than paths and, for each text, adds up the probability of every kept path that collapses to it. A wider beam costs more computation and gets closer to the true best text (Hannun, 2017).

Real systems also add a **language model** (a model of which word sequences are likely) to the score, so that "recognise speech" beats "wreck a nice beach" when the audio is ambiguous. CTC leans on this more than the other families, because it does not learn a language model of its own (Jurafsky and Martin; Hannun, 2017).

<Deep title="Worked detail: the two numbers a CTC beam keeps">

For each candidate text the prefix beam search keeps two totals: paths ending in a blank (`pb`) and paths ending in a letter (`pnb`). The split matters for repeats. If the text ends in `l` and the next frame says `l` again, only the `pb` paths may start a new letter l (they have a blank between), and only the `pnb` paths may continue the same l. ## Streaming and offline

An **offline** recogniser sees the whole recording first. A **streaming** recogniser must write words while the person is still talking, so it can use only a little future audio. That limited look-ahead, plus the compute time, is the delay you feel.

Streaming costs accuracy, because the model has less context. One streaming transformer paper (Emformer) reports, on a standard English benchmark, word error rates of 3.01 and 7.09 percent at an average latency of 80 ms, against 2.50 and 5.62 percent at 960 ms. The first number in each pair is for the clean test set and the second for the harder one. Latency is a design choice you trade against accuracy, not a fixed property of a model family.

Whisper itself reads fixed 30-second windows, and the paper handles long recordings by transcribing consecutive windows. That is a batch design: live use needs extra machinery around it.

## Voice activity detection

A **voice activity detector** (VAD) answers one cheap question for each short slice of audio: is anyone speaking? It saves compute by skipping silence, marks where an utterance starts and ends, and keeps non-speech audio away from a recogniser that might handle it badly (see below).

Simple VADs compare loudness with a threshold and fail in noise, where a fan is louder than a whisper. Neural VADs cope far better. The authors of Silero VAD, an open-source one, state that it is about two megabytes and processes a chunk of 30 ms or more in under a millisecond on one CPU thread.

## How Whisper works

Whisper is an attention encoder-decoder, a transformer, trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts collected from the internet. About 117,000 of those hours cover 96 languages other than English, and 125,000 hours are speech with English translations (Radford et al., 2022).

One network does transcription, translation, language identification and the speech/no-speech decision, because they are all just different token sequences. The README for the open release lists model sizes from tiny (39 million parameters) to large (1,550 million), plus a faster "turbo" variant of 809 million (as of 1 November 2026). ## Where it fails

The decoder is a text-generating language model, so its errors differ from old-style mis-hearings. The paper itself lists repeat loops, missing the first or last words of a segment, and "complete hallucination", where the output is unrelated to the audio.

- **Hallucination on silence or non-speech.** A study of Whisper on a set of real recordings (Koenecke et al., 2024, evaluated as of 2023) found that roughly 1 percent of transcriptions contained whole phrases or sentences that were not in the audio, and 38 percent of those hallucinations included explicit harms, such as invented violence or false authority. They were more common for speakers with longer pauses, a common symptom of aphasia. A fluent invented sentence is worse than a typo because a reader trusts it.
- **Repetition loops**, which the paper says happen more often under greedy decoding. Its recipe is beam search with 5 beams and a fallback: retry at a higher sampling temperature (up to 1.0, in steps of 0.2) when the average log probability is below -1 or the text compresses suspiciously well (a gzip ratio above 2.4, which is how a loop looks). It also combines a no-speech probability above 0.6 with that log-probability test to detect silence, and constrains the first timestamp to the first second so the start is not skipped.
- **Language identification.** Whisper chooses the language itself unless told. On the Fleurs benchmark (102 languages) its accuracy was 64.5 percent, and the paper notes this is partly because 20 of those languages had no training data; on the 82 languages it did cover, it reached 80.3 percent. A short clip or a heavy accent can get the wrong language token and a confident wrong output. If you know the language, pass it.
- **Uneven accuracy.** Jurafsky and Martin's survey of results reported around 2023-24 shows about 2 percent word error for audiobook speech and 25.5 percent for four-person dinner parties recorded with distant microphones.

The practical fixes are not exotic. Cut silence before recognition, with a VAD or Whisper's own no-speech check (in the paper's ablation, that check lowered the average word error across seven long-form test sets from 10.6 to 10.2 percent). Set the language. Test on audio that looks like your product, including silent clips.

## Word error rate in one paragraph

ASR is scored with **word error rate** (WER): count the substitutions, deletions and insertions needed to turn the output into the reference (the edit distance), and divide by the number of reference words. Ten reference words with one wrong, one missing and one extra gives 3 / 10 = 0.30. It can exceed 1, treats every word alike (a wrong drug name costs the same as a wrong "the") and depends on how text is normalised first. A full treatment, with a calculator, comes in [measuring audio quality](../measuring-audio-quality/).

## What to remember

- Speech recognition is an alignment problem: the model must map 100 frames a second to a few letters a second without labelled timings.
- CTC adds a blank, then merges repeats and drops blanks. A text's probability is the sum over all its paths, so the best text is not always the best path.
- Beam search keeps several candidate texts and adds up paths that agree; a language model improves it further.
- Attention encoder-decoders such as Whisper are accurate and flexible but read a window at a time. CTC and transducers stream. Streaming trades accuracy for delay.
- VAD is a cheap first stage. Whisper can invent text on silence, loop, and guess the wrong language; mitigate with a VAD, a fixed language and tests on real recordings.
- WER is edit distance over words, and it hides which words matter.
