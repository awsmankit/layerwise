You now know that a chatbot writes plausible text one piece at a time and cannot check itself. This lesson turns that into practical habits, whether you are using these tools at work or about to build with them.

## Seven habits

1. **Treat the output as a draft from a fast, well-read intern.** Often excellent, occasionally confidently wrong. You are the editor.
2. **Verify what matters.** Names, numbers, dates, quotes, links, citations, legal and medical statements, and whether code actually does what it claims. The more costly a mistake, the more you check.
3. **Give it the facts instead of asking it to recall them.** Paste the document and ask about *that*. Asking it to remember an obscure detail invites a guess.
4. **Be specific.** Say who the audience is, what format you want, how long it should be, and what to avoid. Include an example of good output if you have one.
5. **Protect private information.** Do not paste passwords, customer data, health records or confidential documents into a tool your organisation has not approved. Check how a tool stores and uses what you send.
6. **Ask for its reasoning or sources, then check them.** This makes errors easier to spot. It does not make the answer trustworthy by itself, because the explanation can also be invented.
7. **Write down prompts that work.** If you will do the task again, save the prompt and test it on ten varied examples before relying on it.

## Good uses and risky uses

| Good fit | Handle with care |
|---|---|
| First drafts, rewrites, tone changes | Anything published under your name without review |
| Summarising a document you can skim to confirm | Facts about people, companies or events you cannot verify |
| Explaining a concept or code you are learning | Medical, legal and financial decisions |
| Brainstorming and generating options | Exact calculations (use a calculator or code) |
| Extracting structured data you can spot-check | Decisions that affect people, made without human review |

## How to spot an invented answer

Warning signs, none of them proof:

- **A reference you cannot find.** Citations, case names, links and quotes are the classic invention. Look them up yourself.
- **Details that are too neat.** Very specific numbers or names with no source.
- **Confidence without hedging** on something obscure.
- **It changes when you re-ask.** Ask the same question twice. If the facts differ, at least one is wrong.
- **It agrees with your mistake.** Models tend to go along with a false premise in the question. Ask neutrally, or ask it to challenge your assumption.

Before you rely on something, ask yourself: *if this is wrong, what happens, and who finds out?*
