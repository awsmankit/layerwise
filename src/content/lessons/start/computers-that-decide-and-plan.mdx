So far, the computers in these lessons learned from examples. But a computer can also make good choices without learning anything. It can follow rules, ask questions in a smart order, or look ahead in a game.

These ideas are old, and they are still everywhere. The route in a map app and the opponent in a game can both be built from them.

## Rules

**Imagine this:** a school rule says, "If it rains, we play indoors." Nobody asks the clouds for their opinion. The teacher just checks the sky and follows the rule. A **rule** says "if this, then that". Remember the spam filter from the first lesson, with its rule about the word WINNER? A helper made only of rules like that is called **rule-based**.

Try the helper below. It has three rules. Pick a type of weather and a temperature.

<RuleHelper client:visible />

Rules are easy to read and easy to check. But a person must think of every case. Pick snowy and warm, and no rule fits. Real rule lists keep growing, which is why many jobs moved to learning from examples.

In one line: rules are clear, but they only cover what a person thought of.

## Twenty questions

You know the game. Someone thinks of an animal, and you ask yes or no questions to find it. A **decision tree** is a plan that says which question to ask next, depending on each answer.

A good tree asks questions that cut the animals in half. A bad one wastes questions. Build one below, or let the computer finish it for you.

<AnimalTree client:visible />

Look at how many questions each animal needed. A question that every animal answers the same way tells you nothing. The best first question here splits seven animals into four and three.

Here you picked the questions. A computer can also work them out from examples. That is learning, and it is how many real decision trees are made.

## Looking ahead

Now a game. Five sticks lie on the table. You take 1 or 2 sticks on your turn. Whoever takes the last stick wins.

**Imagine this:** a cricket captain at the end of an over. If I bring on the spinner, the batter may hit out. If he does, I need a fielder at long-on. If I move him there, a gap opens at cover. The captain looks a few steps ahead before moving anyone. (Real captains also shout at the fielders, which the computer skips.) Before it moves, the computer does the same, playing the game in its head, like a chess player thinking "if I do this, you do that". It builds a **look-ahead tree**: each move, then each reply, then each reply to that, until the game ends. Trying all these paths is called **search**. Then it picks a move that wins whatever you do.

<NimLookAhead client:visible />

Play it twice, once going first and once going second. Can you ever win against it? Hint: which pile of sticks is bad for whoever has to move? Look at the blue and yellow circles and squares to see why. A square is blue when every move you have still leads to a computer win.

The computer is not learning here. It simply checks every possible game, so it needs no examples at all.

## Too many games to check

**Imagine this:** checking every page of a thin notebook takes a minute. Checking every page of every book in a huge library takes more than a lifetime. The take-away game is the notebook: it is tiny, with 20 positions in the tree. Tic-tac-toe is small too, with 26,830 possible games when you ignore rotations and mirror images. A computer can check them all.

Chess is different. One well-known estimate says there are at least a 1 followed by 120 zeros of possible games, far more than any computer could check. So chess programs look only a few moves ahead. They use a score to judge how good each position looks.

<Callout title="Three ideas to remember">
Rules: a person writes them, and they miss cases. Trees: a plan of questions, best when each one splits the choices evenly. Search: look ahead, and pick the move that leads to a win.
</Callout>

## Thinking and learning together

Many real systems mix these ideas with learning. A game program might use search to look ahead, and learning to judge how good each position is. The ideas work as a team.

<Deep title="For grown-ups: the technical names">

Rule lists written by experts were the basis of expert systems, which Wikipedia describes as systems that reason through a body of knowledge represented mainly as if-then rules. They were created in the 1970s and widely used in the 1980s, before neural networks took over many jobs.

The tree builder uses a greedy rule for "let the computer finish it": at each node, pick the question whose larger half is smallest. Learned decision trees use a similar greedy idea, but they score splits with a measure such as Gini impurity or entropy, using labelled examples. For this animal set and these five questions, a brute-force search shows the best possible tree has a longest chain of 4 questions and an average of 3.0, and the even-split rule matches that. The animal answers are simplified examples. [Decision trees and random forests](../../ml/decision-trees-and-random-forests/) covers the learned version.

The take-away game is a subtraction game, and the look-ahead tree is solved by minimax: a position is won for the player to move if at least one move leads to a position they win, and lost if every move leads to a win for the other player. The tree for 5 sticks has 20 positions and 8 possible endings. Here a pile that is a multiple of 3 is lost for the player to move, which is why the computer can only be beaten if it must move at 3. Chess-scale search needs pruning, limited depth and an evaluation function; the 10^120 figure is Shannon's conservative lower bound on the game-tree complexity of chess.

</Deep>
