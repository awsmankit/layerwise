Computers only work with numbers. Every photo, song and message on your phone is stored as numbers. So how does a chatbot read the words you type?

It turns them into numbers, in three steps. Think of it as a post office: first cut the letter up, then number each piece, then work out where it lives.

## Step one: cut the text into pieces

**Imagine this:** you cut a big sandwich into bites. A small piece stays whole, and a huge one gets split. The chatbot does this to your text, and the bites are called **tokens**. (Unlike a sandwich, the bites are never eaten.) A token is often a whole word, and sometimes only part of one. Common words get one piece each. Rare words and names get split.

"I love playing football with my friends." is eight pieces: seven words and the full stop. In "My dog is called Biscuit.", the name is split into Bisc and uit. Biscuit is not a common enough name to get its own piece.

The very long word gets cut into ten pieces. A dot in front of a piece means a space came before it.

## Step two: each piece gets a number

The chatbot keeps a long list of every piece it knows, a bit like a dictionary. Each piece has its own number in that list. Here is "What is the capital of France?" as real numbers. They come from the piece-cutting tool (the tokenizer) that some ChatGPT models use.

| Piece | What | ·is | ·the | ·capital | ·of | ·France | ? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 4827 | 382 | 290 | 9029 | 328 | 10128 | 30 |

**Imagine this:** a cricket team where every player wears a jersey number. Number 7 tells you nothing about how that player bats. It only says who is who. In the same way, 9029 says nothing about what "capital" means. It is just a place in the list, like a locker number at school.

## Step three: words get an address on a map

So each piece also gets a second set of numbers. **Imagine this:** in a library, the dog books sit right beside the cat books, far from the books on engines. Where a book sits tells you what it is about. Our map works like that, with an address for every piece. Words with similar meanings live close together. Cat lives near dog, apple near banana, and car near bus.

Nobody places the words by hand. The computer learns the addresses during training, from how words are used. "I fed my cat" and "I fed my dog" are similar sentences, so cat and dog end up as neighbours.

Experts call this address an **embedding**: a list of numbers that says where a word sits on the map of meanings. Tap a word below to see which words live next door.

## Why this matters: chatbots count in pieces

**Imagine this:** a bus charges per stop, not per kilometre. Many AI services count your messages in tokens, not words. One chat can only hold so many tokens, a bit like a page that can only fit so many lines. Apps that pay for AI are often charged per token too.

Rare words, names and some languages need more pieces. So the same message can cost more in one language than in another.

In one line: chatbots read and count in pieces, not in words.

## Why this matters: letter puzzles are hard

Ask a chatbot how many r's are in "strawberry". It does not see ten letters. It sees three pieces: st, raw and berry.

It never looks at the letters one by one, so it can miscount. It is like asking you to count the stripes on a flag you only saw for a second. Newer chatbots often get this one right, but similar letter puzzles can still trip them up.

## Why this matters: searching by meaning

Similar meanings sit close together on the map. So an app can look near your search and find things that mean the same, not just things that match the same words. Search for "puppy" and you might find a page about young dogs, even if it never says "puppy".

<Deep title="For grown-ups: tokenizers and embeddings">

The pieces above come from o200k_base, the tokenizer used by GPT-4o and later OpenAI models. It is built with byte-pair encoding: start from single bytes and repeatedly merge the most frequent pairs, ending with a vocabulary of about 200,000 tokens. Other model families use their own tokenizers, so the same text splits differently.

Each token ID picks out one row of the model's embedding table, a learned vector of hundreds or thousands of numbers. Closeness is usually measured with cosine similarity. Inside the model, these vectors are then adjusted by the surrounding words, so "bank" by a river and "bank" with money end up in different places.

</Deep>
