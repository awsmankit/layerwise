"What job will I do when I grow up?" is a fair question. With AI in the news, it feels bigger than before. Nobody can answer it for certain. This lesson shows what we do know, and lets you look at some real jobs.

## Four kinds of AI work

Most AI work is not about building robots. It comes in four kinds.

- **Building it:** writing the code, training the models and designing the apps.
- **Checking it:** testing AI for mistakes and unfair results.
- **Teaching it:** labelling examples so a computer can learn, like the drawing game in [How a computer learns from examples](../how-computers-learn/).
- **Using it:** nurses, teachers, farmers and almost every other kind of worker, for whom AI is one more tool.

## Jobs are made of tasks

A job is not one thing. A nurse's day is full of small jobs, called **tasks**: taking a reading, calming a parent, writing notes. AI is good at some tasks and poor at others.

So researchers look at tasks, not whole jobs. In 2025 the International Labour Organization studied nearly 30,000 tasks. It found about one worker in four is in a job where AI could do some of the tasks. It says most of those jobs are more likely to change than to vanish, because people are still needed.

That is an estimate about exposure, not a promise about what will happen.

## What helps

No one needs every skill. These come up again and again on the cards.

- **Curiosity.** Wanting to know why something went wrong.
- **Numbers.** School maths is enough to start.
- **Clear writing.** Explaining things so others understand.
- **Clear thinking.** Including checking your own answer.
- **Knowing a field.** Health, farming, law, music, sport.
- **Working with people.** Listening and being kind.
- **Patience and care.** Many jobs are about getting small things right.

## How people get in

There is no single road. Some people study computing, maths or data at university. Some learn a field first, such as biology or accounting, and add data skills later. Some learn by building projects and taking short courses. Research jobs usually ask for longer study. In the US data on the cards, research scientists typically need a master's degree.

Free ways to try it: [Elements of AI](https://www.elementsofai.com) needs no maths or programming to start. [Kaggle Learn](https://www.kaggle.com/learn/intro-to-machine-learning) has short Python and machine-learning courses. [Experience AI](https://experience-ai.org/en/units) has lesson plans, videos and hands-on projects.

## What nobody knows

Nobody knows which jobs will exist in 2040. Government statisticians in the US publish a projection every year. The latest, for 2025 to 2035, came out in August 2026. It expects total jobs to grow 3.5 percent. It also says AI may raise demand for some jobs and lower it for others, and that the uncertainty about AI's effects "remains very high".

It does name one group that is projected to shrink: office and administrative support, down 4.0 percent. The statisticians link that partly to automation tools, including AI. So some tasks and some jobs will change. A projection is a careful guess under stated assumptions. It is not a promise.

## Try it for yourself

You do not have to wait until you are older.

- **Build something small.** Teach the doodle game a new drawing. Notice what it gets wrong.
- **Test an AI tool on purpose.** Ask things you already know. Keep a list of its mistakes.
- **Join a club.** If your school has a coding, maker or science club, try it.
- **Ask people who do the work.** Ask a nurse, a farmer, a teacher: "What do you do all day? What is hard?"
