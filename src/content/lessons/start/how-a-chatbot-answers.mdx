When you press enter, the chatbot does not look up an answer. It writes one, a small piece at a time. Understanding that single fact explains most of the strange behaviour people notice.

## What happens when you press enter

1. Your message, and the conversation so far, is chopped into small pieces called **tokens**. A token is a word or part of a word.
2. The model reads all of those tokens and works out **how likely each possible next token is**. For "The capital of France is", the token " Paris" gets a very high probability.
3. One token is **chosen** from those probabilities.
4. That token is added to the text.
5. Go back to step 2 with the longer text. Stop when the model decides the answer is finished.

That loop is the whole mechanism. Every sentence a chatbot has ever written came out of it, one token at a time.

## What you just saw

**Why the same question gets different answers.** Choosing among likely words involves a bit of chance, like rolling a loaded die. Run it twice and you can get two different, equally reasonable sentences. In the factual example, " Paris" is so likely that it wins nearly every time. In the open-ended one, many words are plausible, so the results vary.

**What temperature does.** It controls how boldly the model chooses. Low temperature makes it stick to the most likely word, which suits factual or precise work. High temperature gives unlikely words a real chance, which suits brainstorming but also produces more mistakes.

<Deep title="The arithmetic">

Temperature reshapes the probabilities. If a token has probability p, its new probability is proportional to p raised to the power 1/T, and then all the values are rescaled to add up to 1. At T below 1, the gap between likely and unlikely tokens widens. At T above 1, it narrows. The demo uses exactly this calculation. Real systems often add other controls too, such as only considering the top few likely tokens.

</Deep>

**Why it sometimes makes things up.** The model's job is to produce a *plausible* continuation, not to check facts. If you ask about something it learned well, the plausible answer is also the true one. If you ask for a detail it never saw, such as a specific court case, a statistic or a reference, it still produces something that *looks* right, because looking right is what it was trained to do. This is called a **hallucination**.

**Why it can seem to know so much.** It was trained on a huge amount of text, and predicting that text well meant absorbing many patterns and facts. It is a compressed, imperfect record of what it read, not a database.

**Why it forgets between chats.** It only sees the text in front of it, the current conversation. The amount of text it can see at once is called its **context window**. Anything outside that window, including an earlier conversation, simply is not there unless the product pastes it back in.

<Callout title="The practical rule">

Treat a chatbot like a fast, well-read colleague who writes fluently and sometimes bluffs. Great for drafts and ideas. Check anything that matters. The next lesson turns that into habits.

</Callout>

## Check your understanding

- Why does the factual example nearly always give "Paris" but the weather example varies?
- What would you set the temperature to for writing a legal clause, and why? What about for brainstorming product names?
- A model gives you a perfect-looking citation. What is the right response?

<Disclosure client:visible title="Show suggested answers">

- **Paris versus the weather.** For the capital of France, one word is far more likely than any other, so even with some chance involved it wins nearly every time. For the weather, many words are about equally plausible, so chance picks different ones.
- **Temperature.** Low for a legal clause, because you want the most likely, precise wording and the same result each time. Higher for brainstorming names, because unusual words are exactly what you want there.
- **The citation.** Look it up yourself. A citation that looks perfect is exactly what a model produces when it is guessing, so only the source itself can confirm it.

</Disclosure>
