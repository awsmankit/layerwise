When you ask a chatbot a question, it does not look up the answer in a big book. It writes a brand new answer, one small piece at a time. Knowing that one fact explains most of the odd things chatbots do.

## What happens when you press enter

1. The chatbot reads your message, and the chat so far.
2. It works out how likely each possible next word is. After "The capital of France is", the word "Paris" is very likely.
3. It picks one word.
4. It adds that word to the text.
5. It goes back to step 2 with the longer text. It stops when the answer looks finished.

That loop is the whole trick. Every sentence a chatbot writes comes out of it, one piece at a time.

Strictly, it works with small pieces of text: often whole words, sometimes parts of words. The [lesson after this one](../how-computers-read-words/) explains them.

## Why answers change

Picking the next word involves a little luck. Imagine a hat full of raffle tickets. Likely words have lots of tickets, unlikely words have only a few, and the chatbot pulls one out.

So if you ask the same thing twice, you can get two different answers.

In the France example, "Paris" has almost all the tickets, so it nearly always wins. In the weather example, lots of words have a fair share, so the result changes more.

## What temperature does

**Temperature** is a setting that controls how bold the chatbot is when it picks a word. At a low temperature, the favourite word gets even more of the tickets. So the chatbot nearly always picks it, which suits facts and careful work.

At a high temperature, the tickets are shared out more evenly. Unlikely words get a real chance. That is good for silly story ideas, but it also leads to more mistakes.

<Deep title="The arithmetic">

Temperature reshapes the probabilities. If a token has probability p, its new probability is proportional to p raised to the power 1/T, and then all the values are rescaled to add up to 1. At T below 1, the gap between likely and unlikely tokens widens. At T above 1, it narrows. The demo uses exactly this calculation. Real systems often add other controls too, such as only considering the top few likely tokens.

</Deep>

## Why it sometimes makes things up

A chatbot's job is to write something that sounds right. It does not check facts. If you ask about something it learned well, what sounds right is usually also true.

But ask for a detail it never saw, like an exact date, and it still writes something that sounds right. That is what it was trained to do. A made-up answer that sounds true is called a **hallucination**.

<Deep title="When made-up answers cause real trouble">

In 2023, two lawyers in New York handed a court a document that quoted earlier court cases. A chatbot had invented several of those cases, and the lawyers had not checked. The judge fined them. Made-up references, quotes and statistics are the most common kind of hallucination, because they look exactly like the real thing.

</Deep>

## Why it seems to know so much

A chatbot read a huge amount, and soaked up lots of facts on the way. But it is not a library where each fact sits on a shelf. It is more like a blurry memory of everything it read.

## Why it forgets between chats

A chatbot can only see the text in front of it: the chat you are having now. The amount of text it can see at once is called its **context window**. An old chat sits outside that window, so the chatbot cannot see it unless the app copies it back in.

<Callout title="The simple rule">

Think of a chatbot as a fast, well-read helper who sometimes bluffs. It is great for first drafts and ideas. Check anything that matters.

</Callout>

## Check your understanding

- Why does the France example nearly always give "Paris", but the weather example keeps changing?
- Would you choose a low or a high temperature for a science fact sheet? What about for funny names for a new pet?
- A chatbot gives you a book title and its author for your project. What should you do?

<Disclosure client:visible title="Show suggested answers">

- **Paris and the weather.** "Paris" has nearly all the tickets, so it nearly always wins. For the weather, many words have a fair share, so chance picks different ones.
- **Temperature.** Low for the fact sheet, to get the most likely words. Higher for pet names, where unusual words are what you want.
- **The book.** Look it up in a library catalogue or on a trusted website. A made-up book can look just as real as a true one.
