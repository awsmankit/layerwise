Think about a normal day. You wake up, check a phone, travel somewhere, watch something, and maybe chat with a bot. Some of that is AI. Some of it only looks clever.

This lesson helps you tell the difference.

## Three words that get mixed up

- **Automation** means a machine follows fixed steps that a person wrote. A kettle that switches off when the water boils is automation.
- **AI** means a model that learned from examples. Nobody wrote its rules by hand. Training found them.
- A **robot** is a machine that moves in the real world.

A robot can follow fixed steps. It can use AI. It can do both. So a robot is a kind of body, and AI is a way of deciding. They are different things.

## Sensing is not thinking

Think of a shop door that opens as you walk up. A **sensor** notices movement and gives the door a signal. The rule is: if something moves, open.

The door has no idea that it is you. It did not learn anything. It is clever to watch, but it is plain automation.

AI often turns up when a sensor's signal needs recognising. Is that a face? What words are in that sound? Researchers call this perception, which means finding meaning in what the sensors pick up.

## Where the data comes from

Every AI thing in the list needed examples to learn from. Many of them also use your data when you use them. Face unlock uses a picture of your face. A maps app uses how phones are moving on the roads. Suggestions use what people click.

Some of this stays on your phone, and some goes to computers owned by a company. Settings let you choose some of it. You can find out more in [AI and your private life](../../responsible/ai-and-your-private-life/).

## Why it matters which one it is

Plain rules do the same thing every time, so you can predict them. AI can be wrong in surprising ways. Automatic subtitles can mishear a name, and YouTube itself warns that accents and noise cause mistakes.

So when a mistake would matter, check the result. That means checking a route before a long trip, and reading subtitles for a school project.

## Why "usually"

The same kind of thing can be built in different ways. Some thermostats are one simple rule. Others learn your habits. A washing machine can be a fixed list of steps. Its maker may also add AI to the name.

When you are not sure, ask two questions. What did it learn from? And what data does it use?

1. Automation follows rules a person wrote. AI follows rules that training found.
2. A robot is a body. It may or may not have AI inside.
3. Sensing is not thinking. A sensor gives numbers, and it takes more to find meaning.

<Deep title="For grown-ups: what is on the phone, and what is in the cloud">

Where the work happens varies. Apple says Face ID matching runs on the device, in the Secure Enclave and its neural engine. Gmail's spam filtering runs on Google's servers. In a February 2019 post, Google said its machine-learning models, together with other protections, block more than 99.9 per cent of spam, phishing and malware.

Recommendation systems have a long history. Amazon's best-known early method, from a 2003 paper, looks at how related products are to each other, instead of matching you to similar customers. Whether a given feed today uses that, or something newer, depends on the company.

Washing machines have used "fuzzy logic" controllers, which are rules with soft edges rather than learning. The word "AI" on a product page is a marketing choice as much as a technical one. Ask what the model is, what it was trained on, and whether it keeps learning from you.
