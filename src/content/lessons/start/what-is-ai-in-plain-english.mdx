People talk about AI all the time. It is in phones, games and homework apps. This lesson explains what it really is, in plain words. You do not need to know anything about computers first.

## A job for a computer

Spam is junk email, like messages saying you have won a prize you never entered. Imagine you want a computer to spot spam and hide it. There are two ways to do it.

## Way one: write the rules

You could write the rules yourself. "If the email says WINNER in capital letters, it is spam." "If it asks for a password, it is spam."

This works for a while. Then the spammers change their words, and your rules miss them. You end up writing new rules for ever.

## Way two: learn from examples

Or you could collect thousands of emails that people have already marked "spam" or "not spam". Then you let the computer find the patterns that tell the two apart. Nobody writes the rules.

When spammers try new tricks, you show the computer new examples. This second way is called **machine learning**: the computer learns the rules from examples. Nearly everything called AI today works like this.

| | Writing the rules | Learning from examples |
|---|---|---|
| Who works out the rules? | A person | The computer, from examples |
| Something new turns up | Only works if someone planned for it | Often works, if it looks like the examples |
| Weak spot | Never finished | Needs good examples, and its mistakes can be hard to explain |

## What a model is

A **model** is the part of an AI system that has learned something. Picture a machine covered in millions of tiny dials. Each way of setting the dials gives different answers.

Before it learns anything, the dials are set at random. So at first, its answers are nonsense.

## How a model learns

**Training** is how the dials get set, by learning from examples. The model sees an example and makes a guess. Then every dial is turned a tiny bit, so the next guess is a little better.

This happens millions of times. Slowly the dials settle where they capture the patterns in the examples.

It is a bit like practising penalty kicks. Each miss tells you to aim a little differently. After thousands of kicks, you hit the target far more often.

Once training is over, the dials stay where they are. They do not change while you chat. The [next lesson](../how-computers-learn/) lets you turn a dial yourself.

<Deep title="Grown-up words for the same ideas">

The dials are called **parameters**, or **weights**. They are just numbers stored in a file, which is why a model can be copied and run on different computers. A model with "8 billion parameters" has 8 billion of these numbers.

Using a trained model is called **inference**. Training is expensive because it needs many powerful chips running for weeks. Each single use is far cheaper, but it adds up when millions of people use a model. Some companies keep chats and may use them to train later versions, which is one reason to read a tool's privacy settings.

</Deep>

## What a chatbot does

The models behind chatbots are called **large language models**, because they learn from a huge amount of writing. Their main training task sounds simple: look at some text and guess what comes next.

To guess well, a model has to pick up grammar, facts and styles of writing. Answering questions and writing stories both grow out of that one skill.

<Deep title="It is not only next-word guessing">

After that first, huge round of training, chat models get extra training. They are shown example conversations, and people rate which answers are more helpful and safe. This turns a good text-guesser into something that behaves like a helpful assistant. Underneath, it still writes by guessing what comes next.

</Deep>

## Good at, and not so good at

| Usually good at | Usually weak at |
|---|---|
| Writing and rewriting text | Facts it never saw, or news since it was trained |
| Explaining an idea in different ways | Exact sums and long chains of careful thinking |
| Summing up a long piece of writing | Saying "I don't know" when it does not know |
| Coming up with ideas | Jobs where one small mistake matters, unless you check |
| Sorting and labelling things | Remembering you from one chat to the next, unless the app saves it |

<Callout title="Three things to remember">

1. It can be wrong and still sound sure. Writing that sounds good is not proof that it is true.
2. It only knows what it learned, plus what is in the chat. It does not look things up unless the app is built to.
3. How you ask matters. A clear question with details gets a better answer than a vague one.

</Callout>
