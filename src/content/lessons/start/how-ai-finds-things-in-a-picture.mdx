You spot a cat in a photo in a blink. For a computer, the same job is hard. Why?

In this lesson you will see how it can be done. Then you will play detective yourself.

## A photo is a grid of numbers

**Imagine this:** a cross-stitch pattern. From far away it is a picture of a cat. Up close it is just rows of tiny coloured stitches. A photo works the same way. Zoom far into a photo on your phone and you see little coloured squares. These tiny dots are called **pixels**. Each pixel has a number for how bright it is. Black is a small number and white is a big one.

So the computer does not see a cat. It sees a huge grid of numbers.

## It hunts for small patterns first

**Imagine this:** you hunt for the word "CAT" in a word search. You do not look for the whole word at once. You scan for the letter C first, then check the letters beside it. The computer also starts small. Looking for a whole "cat" straight away is too big a jump. So the computer starts with tiny patterns, like a line where dark meets light. That line is called an **edge**.

It slides a small pattern-spotter across the whole picture, like a magnifying glass moving over a page. It marks every place where the pattern fits. One spotter hunts for up-and-down edges. Another hunts for sideways ones.

## Then it adds the patterns up

**Imagine this:** a Lego cat. Single bricks make a leg, bricks make a tail, and legs, tail and head make the cat. (Nobody has ever stepped on a pixel, which is a point in their favour.) Small patterns join up into bigger ones in the same way. Edges make corners and curves. Curves and corners make an ear, an eye or a whisker. Ears, eyes and whiskers together make a cat's face.

Nobody types in these patterns. The computer finds the useful ones by itself, by training on lots of examples, just like the fruit game in [How a computer learns](../how-computers-learn/).

## Try it: the pixel detective

This detective is much simpler than a real one. It knows only four shapes: a plus, a cross, a square and a letter L. Tap squares to shade them, or pick a picture to start from.

First it looks for **small patterns**: three shaded squares in a row. Then it checks which shape those short lines add up to. It also says how sure it is.

<PixelDetective client:visible />

Things to try:

- Start with "A plus". Then move it to a different place. The detective still finds it.
- Pick "A plus with one square missing". Its sureness drops a little.
- Pick "A plus with a gap in the middle". A person still sees a plus. The detective finds nothing. Why?
- Pick "A plus and a cross on top of each other". Watch it get confused.

## Drawing a box and saying how sure

When the detective finds a shape, it draws a dashed **box** around it. Real detectors do the same, and they give each box a **confidence** score. It is a number for how sure the computer is, such as 90 out of 100.

**Imagine this:** a friend says, "I am 90 percent sure that is Meena's bike." You still glance at the bell. The score is the **confidence**, and it is only how sure the guesser feels. A high score is not a promise. A confident computer can still be wrong, just like a confident person.

## Why it gets fooled

**Imagine this:** you spot a friend in a crowd by their red backpack, but today a stranger wears the same one. You would be fooled until you saw the face. Computers lean on small clues like that too. The detective gave up when you left a gap. Real systems are far better than that. But they still have blind spots. A shadow, a strange angle or a half-hidden object can confuse them.

Researchers have found that tiny changes can fool a computer. People would not even notice these changes.

In one experiment, black and white stickers were put on a real stop sign. A road-sign program the researchers had built then read it as a different sign. People still saw a stop sign.

That is why people check results that matter, especially for safety.

## Where you meet it

- **Phone cameras.** Face boxes that pop up when you take a photo are made by face detection. It finds faces. It does not know who they are.
- **Photo search.** Tools can label what is in your photos, such as "dog" or "beach", so you can search for them.
- **Self-checkouts.** Some shops are trying cameras that spot loose fruit and vegetables and suggest them on the screen.

<Deep title="For grown-ups: how this toy relates to real detectors">

The detective uses hand-written rules: runs of three shaded squares as "parts", and a fixed list of parts for each shape. Real systems learn their patterns. A convolutional neural network slides small learned filters over the image, which is what the "small pattern-spotters" are. Early layers tend to respond to edges and colour patches, and later layers to larger structures, as shown in the Zeiler and Fergus visualisations. The deeper lesson on this is [How computers see](../../deep-learning/how-computers-see/).

The detective's sliding of a shape over every position is a simplified version of how detectors search a picture, and its box is a bounding box. Real detectors also choose between many overlapping boxes and measure how well a box fits the true object. That is covered in [Detection and segmentation](../../deep-learning/detection-and-segmentation/).

The confidence here is just found parts divided by parts needed plus extras. In real models the score comes from the network, and it is not automatically a calibrated probability, so 90 per cent does not always mean right nine times in ten.

The sticker result is from Eykholt et al. (2017), tested on their own road-sign classifiers. Szegedy et al. (2013) first reported that tiny, deliberately chosen changes to an image can change a network's answer. Today's systems are trained to resist some of this, but it has not gone away. The self-checkout line is based on a trade article dated 27 May 2026, which describes the idea and its challenges rather than proving any particular shop's results.
