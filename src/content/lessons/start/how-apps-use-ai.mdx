You now know how a chatbot writes its answer. But the chat app on your phone is not the model itself. It is an app that talks to a model. This lesson shows how that works.

## The model and the app are different

The model is the part that learned. It is really a very big file of numbers, plus code to run it. Give it text, and it writes more text.

It has no buttons, no login and no memory of yesterday.

The app is everything around it: the screen, your account, your saved chats and the rules about what the bot may say. Many apps can use the same model behind the scenes.

## Sending a message to the model

Big models need very powerful computers, so most apps do not run them on your phone. Instead, the app sends your message over the internet to a company that runs the model. Then it gets the answer back.

The app asks through an **API**, a set of rules for how one program asks another for something. API is short for application programming interface.

## The waiter

Think of a restaurant. You do not walk into the kitchen. You tell the waiter what you want, and the waiter brings your food.

The API is the waiter, and the model is the kitchen. The app shows a secret key, a bit like a membership card, so the company knows who is ordering. And like a restaurant, there is a bill for every order.

## What the model actually reads

Say you type "Can I change my delivery address?" into a shop's help chat. The model gets much more than that. The app builds a bigger package called the **prompt**, which is everything the model reads before it answers. It usually holds:

- Instructions from the app's makers, such as "Be polite. Keep answers short."
- The whole chat so far.
- Sometimes, pages from the shop's own help guide.
- Your new message, at the end.

## Why it seems to remember

The model remembers nothing between messages. So each time you reply, the app sends the whole chat again. That is why the chatbot seems to remember.

It is also why very long chats get slower. They can even hit the limit of what the model can read at once.

## The open-book exam

A model only knows what it learned in training. It has never seen a shop's newest price list. So how can a help bot answer questions about it?

Usually, the app does what you would do in an open-book exam. Before answering, it finds the right pages and keeps them open on the desk.

1. The app keeps its documents, such as guides and rules, where it can search them.
2. When you ask a question, it finds the few passages that match best.
3. It pastes them into the prompt with an instruction like "Answer using only this text."
4. The model writes its answer from the pages in front of it.

The good part is that new facts only need new pages, not a new model. The weak part is the search. If the app does not find the right page, the model may guess instead.

<Callout title="A useful test">

When an app answers a question about your school's or club's rules, ask where the answer came from. If it cannot show you the page, treat it as unchecked.

</Callout>

<Deep title="The grown-up name: RAG">

This open-book pattern is called **retrieval-augmented generation**, or **RAG**: "retrieval" for the lookup, "generation" for the writing. Ahead of time, the app splits its documents into short passages and stores them in a searchable index. At question time it pulls out the most related passages and adds them to the prompt. A big advantage is that the app can show you which passage an answer came from. Its weak point is the search step: a passage that is not found cannot be used.

</Deep>

<Deep title="What fine-tuning means">

**Fine-tuning** is different. It takes an existing model and trains it a bit more on a smaller set of your own examples, which changes the model's weights. It is good for teaching a steady style, format or narrow skill, such as always writing in a company's tone or sorting support tickets into fixed groups. It is a poor way to add facts that change, because every change means training again, and the model can still mix up what it learned. Most apps try better prompts first, then retrieval, and fine-tune only when those are not enough.

</Deep>

## Why answers cost money and time

Every piece of text the model reads or writes takes real work on expensive computer chips. So:

- Long prompts cost more. The model reads the instructions and the whole chat every time.
- Long answers take longer. The model writes one small piece after another.
- Bigger models cost more. They are often better, but slower, so many apps use a small model for easy jobs.

<Deep title="How the bill works">

Model providers usually charge **per token**, the small pieces of text from the earlier lesson. There is one price for the tokens sent in, and a separate, usually higher, price for the tokens the model writes back. The instructions at the top of the prompt, often called the **system prompt**, are sent and billed on every request, and so is the whole chat history. Prices differ widely between models and change often, so always check the provider's current price page.

</Deep>

<Deep title="Why apps rarely run the model themselves">

Running a large model means keeping all of its weights in the fast memory of specialised chips, usually GPUs. An 8-billion-parameter model stored at 16-bit precision is about 16 GB of numbers before it has read a single word, and the strongest models are far larger. Renting or buying that hardware and keeping it busy is a job in itself, so most apps pay a provider per token instead. Some organisations do run open models themselves, for privacy, cost at high volume, or offline use. The [Local AI track](../../local/run-models-locally/) covers that path.

</Deep>
