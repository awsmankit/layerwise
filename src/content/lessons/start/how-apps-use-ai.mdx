You now know that a chatbot writes its answer one token at a time. But the chat window, a writing assistant in your email or a support bot on a website are not the model itself. They are apps that talk to a model. This lesson shows how that conversation works, with no code needed.

## The model and the app are different things

A **model** is, in the end, a very large file of numbers (the "weights" that training set) plus some code that knows how to run them. Give it text, and it produces more text. That is all it does. It has no buttons, no login, no memory of yesterday, and no idea which company is using it.

The **app** is everything around it: the screen you type into, your account, the saved chat history, the rules about what the bot should and should not do, and the code that decides what to send to the model. Many different apps can use the same model. Two products that feel very different may be sending text to the same model behind the scenes.

## What an API is

Most apps do not run the model on your phone or on their own computers. They send a request over the internet to a company that runs the model on powerful chips, and they get an answer back. The agreed way of making that request is called an **API** (application programming interface).

Think of a restaurant. You do not walk into the kitchen. You tell the waiter what you want, in the format the menu allows, and the waiter brings back the dish. The API is the waiter: it takes a request in an agreed format, passes it to the kitchen (the model), and brings back the result. The app holds a secret key that identifies who is ordering, so the provider knows whom to bill.

And there is a bill. Model providers usually charge **per token**: a price for the tokens sent in, and a separate, usually higher, price for the tokens the model writes back. Prices differ widely between models and change often, so always check the provider's current price page.

## What a prompt really contains

When you type "Can I change my delivery address?" into a shop's support bot, the model does not receive just that sentence. The app builds a bigger package called the **prompt**. It usually contains:

- **Instructions** written by the app's makers, often called a system prompt: "You are the support assistant for this shop. Be brief. Never promise refunds."
- **The conversation so far**, because the model remembers nothing between requests. The app sends the whole chat again every time you reply.
- **Sometimes documents**, such as the relevant part of the shop's delivery policy.
- **Your new message**, at the end.

That is why a chatbot seems to remember what you said five messages ago: the app keeps pasting it back in. It is also why very long chats get slower and more expensive, and eventually hit the limit of how much text the model can read at once.

## Giving a model your own data without retraining it

A model only knows what was in its training data, up to a certain date. It has never seen your company's handbook or last week's price list. So how does a support bot answer questions about them?

Usually not by retraining. Instead the app does what a sensible student does in an open-book exam: before answering, it looks up the relevant pages and keeps them open on the desk.

1. Ahead of time, the app splits its documents into short passages and stores them in a searchable index.
2. When you ask a question, it searches that index for the passages most related to your question.
3. It pastes those passages into the prompt with an instruction such as "Answer using only the text below."
4. The model writes an answer based on the passages in front of it.

This pattern is called **retrieval-augmented generation**, or **RAG**: "retrieval" for the lookup, "generation" for the writing. Its big advantages are that updating knowledge only means updating the documents, and that the app can show you which passage an answer came from. Its weak point is the search: if the right passage is not found, the model cannot use it, and it may guess instead.

<Callout title="A useful test">

When an AI app gives a confident answer about your organisation's rules, ask yourself where that knowledge came from. If the app cannot show you a source passage, treat the answer as unchecked.

</Callout>

## What fine-tuning means

**Fine-tuning** is different: it takes an existing model and continues training it on a smaller set of your own examples, which changes the model's weights. It is good for teaching a consistent style, format or narrow skill, such as always writing in a company's tone or sorting tickets into fixed categories. It is a poor way to add facts that change, because every change means training again, and the model can still mix up what it learned. Most apps reach for better prompts first, then retrieval, and fine-tune only when those are not enough.

## Why answers cost money and take time

Every token the model reads or writes is real computation on expensive specialised chips. Three things follow.

- **Long prompts cost more.** Instructions, the full chat history and pasted documents are all billed as input on every single request.
- **Long answers take longer.** The model writes one token after another, so a long reply simply takes more steps. Reading the prompt also takes time, more for longer prompts.
- **Bigger models cost more.** A larger model usually gives better answers but needs more computation per token, so it is slower and pricier. Many apps use a small model for easy tasks and a large one only when needed.

<Deep title="Why apps rarely run the model themselves">

Running a large model means keeping all of its weights in the fast memory of specialised chips, usually GPUs. An 8-billion-parameter model stored at 16-bit precision is about 16 GB of numbers before it has read a single word, and the strongest models are far larger. Renting or buying that hardware and keeping it busy is a job in itself, so most apps pay a provider per token instead. Some organisations do run open models themselves, for privacy, cost at high volume, or offline use. The [Local AI track](../../local/run-models-locally/) covers that path.

</Deep>

## What to take forward

- The model is a file of numbers that turns text into more text. The app is everything around it.
- Most apps call a model through an API and pay per token, for both what they send and what comes back.
- The prompt is more than your message: instructions, the whole conversation so far, and sometimes documents.
- RAG lets an app use your own documents by finding the relevant passages and pasting them in, with no retraining.
- Fine-tuning changes the model itself. It suits style and narrow skills better than changing facts.
