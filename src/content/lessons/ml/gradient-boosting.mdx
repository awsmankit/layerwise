[Classical ML essentials](../../foundations/classical-ml-essentials/) calls gradient-boosted trees "the baseline to beat" for tables of data, and stops there. This lesson fills the gap: what boosting is, what its settings do, why the three well-known libraries (XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost) are so often the ones people reach for, and what the evidence really says about boosting versus deep learning.

A random forest, from [the previous lesson](../decision-trees-and-random-forests/), grows many deep trees independently and averages them. Boosting does the opposite. It grows many **small** trees one after another, and each new tree is trained on **what the model so far still gets wrong**.

## The idea, by hand, on five rows

Take five rows with one input x and a number y to predict. The values are made up.

**Round 0.** Start with the dullest model: predict the average of y for everyone. The average of 3, 5, 4, 9 and 11 is 6.4. The **residuals** (actual minus predicted) are -3.4, -1.4, -2.4, 2.6 and 4.6. Total squared error: 47.2.

**Round 1.** Fit a tiny tree, a **stump** with a single question, to the residuals, not to y. The best question splits between x = 3 and x = 4. The left rows have residuals averaging -2.4, the right rows 3.6. Do not add the whole correction. Scale it by a **learning rate**, here 0.5, so the left rows move by -1.2 and the right rows by +1.8.

**Round 2.** Work out the new residuals, fit another stump to them, add half of it, and so on.

| x | y | Round 0 prediction | Residual | Round 1 prediction | Residual | Round 2 prediction | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 6.4 | -3.4 | 5.2 | -2.2 | 4.6 | -1.6 |
| 2 | 5 | 6.4 | -1.4 | 5.2 | -0.2 | 4.6 | 0.4 |
| 3 | 4 | 6.4 | -2.4 | 5.2 | -1.2 | 4.6 | -0.6 |
| 4 | 9 | 6.4 | 2.6 | 8.2 | 0.8 | 9.1 | -0.1 |
| 5 | 11 | 6.4 | 4.6 | 8.2 | 2.8 | 9.1 | 1.9 |
| | | | **47.2** | | **14.8** | | **6.7** |

The bottom row is the total squared error. In round 2 the stump again splits between 3 and 4, with leaf values of -1.2 (left) and +1.8 (right), and at half strength these move the left rows by -0.6 and the right rows by +0.9. Each round the residuals shrink, because each tree is aimed at exactly what is left. The final prediction is the starting average plus the sum of all the scaled trees.

<Deep title="Why it is called gradient boosting">
For squared error, the residual is exactly the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction, so "fit a tree to the residuals" is a step of gradient descent, with the step taken in the space of predictions rather than on a set of weights. That is the link to [How a model learns](../../foundations/how-models-learn/): there each step nudges numbers; here each step adds a tree.

Friedman (2001) generalised this to other losses. For log loss (yes/no questions) or absolute error, each tree is fitted to the negative gradient of that loss, which is no longer the plain residual. This is why one algorithm handles regression, classification and ranking: only the loss, and so the gradient, changes. For a long, careful derivation see the explained.ai article by Parr and Howard.

XGBoost goes a step further. Its documentation describes an objective of training loss plus a penalty on the complexity of the trees, and a second-order approximation of the loss when choosing each tree.
</Deep>

## The knobs that matter

A boosted model has many settings. A handful do most of the work.

| Knob | What it does | If you raise it |
|---|---|---|
| Number of rounds (trees) | How many corrections are added | Fits the training data more closely, and eventually fits noise |
| Learning rate | How much of each tree is added | Fits faster, with a rougher, riskier path; lower needs more rounds |
| Tree depth (or leaf count) | How much one tree can do, and how many inputs it can combine | Each tree gets stronger and can capture interactions, and overfits sooner |
| Row subsampling | Each tree sees a random share of the rows | Adds noise that often reduces overfitting, and speeds training |
| Column subsampling | Each tree or split sees a random share of the inputs | Same idea, applied to inputs |
| Regularisation (for example minimum rows per leaf, penalties on leaf values) | Penalises complex trees | Simpler trees, less overfitting |

Learning rate and number of rounds trade against each other. A smaller learning rate with more rounds is a common recipe, because many small steps are more cautious than a few large ones; the price is training time. Try 1.0 in the figure: the model chases every point at once and reaches its best validation error in a handful of rounds, then gets worse. Try 0.05 and it needs about a hundred.

The libraries differ in defaults, and the defaults are only starting points. The values below are from each project's documentation, checked on 1 November 2026, and may change in later versions:

| Setting | XGBoost | LightGBM |
|---|---|---|
| Learning rate | `eta` (alias `learning_rate`), 0.3 | `learning_rate`, 0.1 |
| Tree size | `max_depth`, 6 | `num_leaves`, 31 (`max_depth` is unlimited by default) |
| Row subsampling | `subsample`, 1 (all rows) | `bagging_fraction`, 1.0 |
| Column subsampling | `colsample_bytree`, 1 | `feature_fraction` |

Boosted models are more sensitive to settings than random forests, which work reasonably out of the box. The explained.ai article makes this point directly. A sensible way to tune: fix a moderate learning rate, use early stopping (next section) to choose the number of rounds, then adjust tree size and subsampling with cross-validation on the training data.

## Early stopping: let the validation set choose the number of rounds

Training error falls with every added tree, which is no guide to when to stop. Validation error falls and then rises, as the bottom chart in the figure shows. **Early stopping** watches it:

1. Train for a large maximum number of rounds, on the training rows.
2. After every round, score the held-back validation rows.
3. Stop when the validation score has not improved for a set number of rounds (the **patience**), and keep the best round.

XGBoost exposes this as `early_stopping_rounds`: the validation metric must improve at least once in that many rounds for training to continue. LightGBM has `early_stopping_round`, which does the same.

With the figure's starting settings (learning rate 0.1, trees of depth 2) the validation error is lowest after 48 trees, at 0.38 mean squared error. By 150 trees the training error has fallen from 0.12 to 0.02 while validation error has crept up to 0.51. These numbers come from this one small made-up dataset, so treat them as an illustration of the shape, not a rule.

<Callout title="The validation set is now part of the model">
Choosing the number of rounds on the validation set means its score is no longer an honest estimate of future performance. Keep a separate test set that touches none of this, as described in [Classical ML essentials](../../foundations/classical-ml-essentials/).
</Callout>

## XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost

All three implement gradient boosted decision trees. The differences are in engineering and in a few ideas, and they are worth knowing when choosing:

- **XGBoost** (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) popularised the approach in competitions. Its paper describes a regularised objective, a sparsity-aware algorithm for missing and sparse data, and systems work for scaling. Its documentation lists a histogram-based tree method, and, since version 1.5, support for categorical data.
- **LightGBM** bins continuous values into histograms, which its documentation says speeds up training and reduces memory. It grows trees **leaf-wise**: always splitting the leaf that reduces loss most, which tends to reach lower loss for the same number of leaves but can overfit on small data, so `num_leaves` and `max_depth` matter. It also has a special split search for categorical inputs.
- **CatBoost** puts categorical inputs at the centre. Its paper describes **ordered boosting** and an algorithm for categorical features, both designed to fight a "prediction shift" that the authors trace to a kind of target leakage in standard boosting.
- **scikit-learn** also provides a histogram-based gradient boosting model, `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` and `HistGradientBoostingRegressor`, if you want no extra install.

Which is best varies by dataset, so run the same cross-validation on two of them with a few settings tried rather than relying on a general ranking.

**Why trees, boosted, do so well on tables.** Tables mix numbers, categories and missing values on different scales. Trees only compare an input with a cut, so they need no scaling, and boosting lets many shallow trees add up to a flexible model. Training is usually quick on an ordinary CPU, and XGBoost's paper describes built-in handling of missing and sparse values. The practical checklist (splits, leakage, metrics) is the same as everywhere else in this track.

Two more ensemble ideas are worth a sentence. **Voting** averages the predictions of several different models. **Stacking** trains a final model that takes the other models' predictions as its inputs. Both are common in competitions, and both depend on the pieces being different enough to disagree usefully.

## Tabular data: boosting versus deep learning

"Boosting beats neural networks on tables" is widely repeated and partly true. What the published comparisons say, in date order:

- **Shwartz-Ziv and Armon (2021)** compared several recently proposed deep models with XGBoost across datasets, including the ones used in the deep models' own papers. They report that XGBoost outperformed the deep models and needed much less tuning, and that an ensemble of the deep models with XGBoost did better than XGBoost alone.
- **Borisov et al. (2021)**, a survey with a five-dataset comparison, report that gradient boosted tree ensembles still mostly outperformed the deep models they tested on supervised tasks.
- **Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022)** benchmarked on 45 datasets and found tree-based models remain state of the art on **medium-sized data of roughly 10,000 samples**, even before counting their speed. Their analysis points to three weaknesses of neural networks on tables: sensitivity to uninformative inputs, losing the orientation of the data, and difficulty fitting irregular functions.
- **McElfresh et al. (2023)** compared 19 algorithms on 176 datasets and conclude the "neural network versus boosted trees" debate is **overemphasised**: for many datasets the gap is negligible, or light tuning of a boosted model matters more than the choice of family. They also find boosted trees much better at skewed, heavy-tailed inputs. A notable exception was TabPFN, a pretrained network, though it was limited to training sets of about 3,000 rows.
- **Hollmann et al. (2025, Nature)** present TabPFN as a tabular foundation model. The authors report that it outperforms previous methods on datasets with up to 10,000 samples, using far less training time. This is a claim from the model's own authors, on small data, and independent comparisons continue to appear.

**An honest summary.** On typical tables, tuned boosted trees are a strong baseline that is hard to beat and cheap to run, which is why they are the baseline to beat. The size of the margin depends on the datasets, the tuning budget and the benchmark chosen, and results from small-data pretrained models are changing the picture at the small end. Neural networks pull ahead when the inputs are images, text or audio, when a table sits beside such inputs in one model, or when data is very large and you want learned representations. And a [linear or logistic regression](../linear-and-logistic-regression/) still deserves a run first: if a boosted model barely beats it, the extra complexity may not be worth it.

## What to remember

- Boosting builds small trees in sequence. Each is fitted to the current residuals and added at a fraction (the learning rate) of full strength.
- In the five-row example, the total squared error falls from 47.2 to 14.8 to 6.7 over two rounds.
- Learning rate and number of rounds trade off: lower rates need more rounds. Pick the rounds with early stopping on a validation set.
- Depth (or leaves), row and column subsampling and regularisation are the other knobs that matter. Defaults differ between libraries.
- XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are all boosted trees. LightGBM is histogram-based and grows leaf-wise, and CatBoost has special handling of categorical inputs.
- On medium-sized tables, boosted trees are the baseline to beat, backed by several benchmarks, but the evidence is about particular datasets and settings, and the gap varies.
