Most of the assistants you use are produced by a sequence of training stages. Knowing which stage does what tells you what a vendor can change in an update, what fine-tuning on your side can realistically achieve, and why a model has a knowledge cutoff.

## What each stage changes

**Pretraining sets what the model knows and how well it can learn in context.** Facts, writing styles, code ability and languages come from here. This is also why a model has a **knowledge cutoff**: it only saw data up to a certain date.

**Instruction and preference tuning mostly change behaviour, not knowledge.** The same pretrained model can feel very different depending on how it was post-trained: terse or chatty, cautious or compliant, good at following a format or not.

**Reasoning training changes how the model uses its output budget.** Some models produce a long chain of intermediate tokens before the final answer. Those tokens take time and are usually billed as output.

<Callout kind="cost" title="Why training cost is not your cost">
Training a frontier model is an enormous one-off expense for the lab. As a user you pay per token for inference instead, and that cost recurs for as long as the feature runs. Budget for the second one.
</Callout>

<Disclosure title="RLHF versus DPO, in one paragraph">

Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a separate reward model from human rankings, then optimises the language model against that reward. Direct preference optimisation skips the reward model and trains the language model directly on pairs of preferred and rejected responses, which is simpler to run. Both turn "people preferred this answer" into a training signal.

</Disclosure>

## What this means when you build

- **A vendor update can change behaviour without changing facts.** A new post-training run can alter format, tone and refusals. That is why you pin model versions and keep an eval suite.
- **Fine-tuning on your side is post-training in miniature.** It is good at style, format and narrow skills. It is a poor way to add large amounts of new knowledge.
- **Scale is a trade-off.** Work on compute-optimal training showed that, for a fixed budget, many models had been trained on too little data for their size. It is one reason smaller models trained on more data can match larger ones.
