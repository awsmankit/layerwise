A sound, a sensor reading or a signal over time often looks like a jumble. The **Fourier transform** asks a different question: which pure waves, at which frequencies, add up to this? Its answer, the **spectrum**, shows each frequency and how strong it is. It is the basis of audio features such as spectrograms, and it appears in many signal and image methods.

## Waves add up

A pure sine wave has one frequency and one amplitude. Add two sine waves together and you get a wave that looks more complicated, but it is still just two waves. The Fourier transform runs the other way: given the combined wave, it recovers the two frequencies and their amplitudes.

## How it works, in one line

For a sampled signal $$x_n$$ of length $$N$$, the discrete Fourier transform gives one number per frequency $$k$$ (the number of whole cycles in the window):

$$
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \, e^{-2\pi i k n / N}
$$

Each term multiplies the signal by a wave at frequency $$k$$. If the signal contains that frequency, the products add up to a large value. If not, they cancel. The size of $$X_k$$, scaled by $$2/N$$, is the amplitude of that frequency in the signal.

<Callout title="Why ML cares">

Speech models often start from a spectrogram, which is a Fourier transform taken over short windows as the signal moves on. Many image models use frequency ideas to filter noise or compress data. Knowing that a spike means a wave at that frequency makes these features easier to read in a paper or a model's code.

## Where it came from

Joseph Fourier introduced the idea in 1807 while studying heat flow, claiming that any periodic function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines. The discrete version used on computers was popularised by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) in 1965, which made the calculation fast enough for everyday use.

<Deep title="Why the spikes are exactly at the right heights">

With $$N$$ samples and a whole number of cycles $$k$$, a pure sine of amplitude $$A$$ gives a Fourier value of $$AN/2$$ at its frequency, and close to zero everywhere else. Scaling by $$2/N$$ turns that into $$A$$ exactly. The widget uses whole cycles, so each spike is clean. Real signals rarely fit whole cycles, which smears the spike into nearby frequencies; that effect is called spectral leakage.

</Deep>

## What to take forward

- The Fourier transform splits a signal into the sine waves that make it up.
- Each frequency in the spectrum has a height equal to the amplitude of that wave.
- The discrete transform is a sum of the signal times waves at each frequency.
- Spectrograms and frequency features rely on this idea, and it explains much of audio processing.
