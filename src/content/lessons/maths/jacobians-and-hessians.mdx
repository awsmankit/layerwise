The gradient handles a function with one output and many inputs. A layer in a network has many outputs too, each depending on many inputs. The **Jacobian** collects every partial derivative for that case into one grid.

## The grid

Take $$F(x, y) = (xy,\; x + y^2)$$. It has two inputs and two outputs. The Jacobian has one row per output and one column per input, and each entry is the partial derivative of an output with respect to an input:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} \partial F_1/\partial x & \partial F_1/\partial y \\ \partial F_2/\partial x & \partial F_2/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y & x \\ 1 & 2y \end{pmatrix}
$$

At $$(x, y) = (1, 2)$$ this is $$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$.

## The best linear guess

Near a point, a small input step $$(\Delta x, \Delta y)$$ changes the output by approximately $$J \cdot (\Delta x, \Delta y)$$. That is a matrix times a vector, the same operation as a neural network layer. The Jacobian is the local version of the layer.

## Try it

Set the point and the step. The predicted change comes from the Jacobian, the exact change from evaluating $$F$$ at both points. Make the step small and the two agree closely. Make it large and the gap grows, because the linear guess only holds locally.

<Callout title="Why ML needs it">

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to Jacobians. Each layer contributes its Jacobian, and the gradient of the loss is the product of those Jacobians applied to the loss's own slope. Frameworks compute these products without forming the full matrices, which is why very large networks remain trainable.

</Callout>

## Where it came from

Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi studied these determinants and matrices in the 1830s, and the name Jacobian was given in his honour. The Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, is named after Ludwig Otto Hesse, who worked on it in the 1840s.

<Deep title="The Hessian in one paragraph">

The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient: the matrix of second partial derivatives $$\partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$$. It describes curvature. At a minimum, the Hessian is positive in every direction, so the surface curves up on all sides. Optimisers that use curvature, such as Newton's method, use it to choose better steps, but for large models the full Hessian is too big to store, so practical methods approximate it.

</Deep>

## Does gradient descent get stuck in local minima?

**Does gradient descent get stuck in local minima?** A point where the gradient is zero is a minimum only if the Hessian curves up in every direction. If it curves up in some directions and down in others, it is a **saddle point**: flat in the gradient, but not a valley. Take $$f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$$ at $$(0, 0)$$. The gradient is $$(2x, -2y) = (0, 0)$$ there, and the Hessian is $$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$$, with eigenvalues 2 and -2. It curves up along $$x$$ and down along $$y$$, so a step along $$y$$ goes downhill.

In high dimensions, a point where every one of thousands of directions curves up is rare, while a mix of up and down is common. Research on large networks (Dauphin et al., 2014) found that the trouble in high dimensions is mostly saddle points and flat plateaus rather than bad local minima. Treat this as an empirical finding for large networks, not a theorem for every model. Gradient descent can slow down near a saddle, and the noise in stochastic gradient descent, or momentum, helps it roll off along a downward direction.

## What to take forward

- A Jacobian is the grid of partial derivatives for a function with several outputs.
- Near a point, it gives the best linear prediction of a small input change.
- Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to Jacobians, layer by layer.
- The Hessian is the next level up, describing curvature.
