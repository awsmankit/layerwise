A **Markov chain** is a sequence of states where the next state depends only on the current one, not on the whole history. Weather, a queue, a random walk and the next word in a simple text model can all be modelled this way. The rule is captured by a **transition matrix**: a table of the chance of moving from each state to each other state.

## The transition matrix

Take two states, sunny and rainy. Suppose a sunny day is followed by sun 90 per cent of the time, and a rainy day by rain 50 per cent of the time. The matrix records those chances, and each row adds up to 1:

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

If today's distribution is a row vector $$\pi$$, tomorrow's is $$\pi P$$. Repeat the multiplication and the probability moves through the chain one step at a time.

## Try it

Start with a certainly sunny day and watch the chance of sun over 20 days. It drops quickly at first and then levels off. The flat level is the **stationary distribution**, and it is the same whatever the starting day. Change how sticky the states are and the flat level moves.

<Callout title="Why language models are chains">

A language model that generates one word at a time is a Markov chain over words if it looks only at the last word. Real models look at a long context, so the chain is far bigger, but the idea of a probability distribution over the next state, given the current one, is the same. ## Where it came from

Andrey Markov introduced the idea in 1906 while studying letter sequences in a Pushkin novel, a question about whether independence was needed for the law of large numbers. The theory became central to physics, queueing, and later to speech recognition and text modelling.

<Deep title="Finding the steady state">

A stationary distribution $$\pi$$ satisfies $$\pi P = \pi$$, together with the requirement that its entries add to 1. For the two-state chain with sunny stays 0.9 and rainy stays 0.5, the balance condition gives $$\pi_s \cdot 0.1 = \pi_r \cdot 0.5$$, so $$\pi_r = 0.2\,\pi_s$$. Adding up gives $$\pi_s = 1/1.2 \approx 0.833$$. The widget's dashed line is this value.

</Deep>

## What to take forward

- A Markov chain's next state depends only on its current state.
- A transition matrix gives the chance of each move, and each row sums to 1.
- Repeated multiplication moves the probability through the chain.
- For many chains the distribution settles to a stationary value that does not depend on the start.
