Integration is the reverse of the derivative. Where the derivative asks for the slope at a point, the integral asks for the **total**: the area under a curve, or a sum of many small pieces. It appears in ML mainly where probabilities are continuous, because a probability for a continuous value is an area.

## Adding strips

The idea is simple. Cut the area under a curve into thin vertical strips, find the height of each, multiply by the strip's width, and add them up. Each strip is a rectangle, and the rectangles together approximate the area. You can use the height at the left edge, the right edge, or the middle. The middle is usually most accurate.

## Try it

The curve is $$f(x) = x^2$$ between 0 and 1. The exact area is $$1/3$$. Move the strip count from 4 to 40 and watch the total close in on 0.3333.

<Callout title="Why thin strips work">

With 4 strips the error is about 0.005; with 40 it is about 0.00005. Each doubling of strips cuts the error roughly four times for this smooth curve. Integration is the limit as the strips become infinitely thin, which the notation $$\int_0^1 x^2 \, dx$$ captures in one symbol.

</Callout>

## Where ML uses it

- **Probabilities for continuous values.** The chance a measurement falls between two values is the area under its density curve, as in the [normal distribution](../normal-distribution/).
- **Expected values for continuous variables.** The expectation is an integral: $$E[X] = \int x \, p(x) \, dx$$, the discrete sum from the [expectation lesson](../expectation-and-variance/) with strips instead of dice faces.
- **Normalising constants.** Making a density integrate to 1 is what turns a shape into a probability model. Many ML methods avoid computing such integrals directly, because they are hard, and use sampling or gradients instead.

## Where it came from

Archimedes found areas by filling shapes with thin slices around 250 BC. Newton and Leibniz linked integration with differentiation in the 1670s, and the link is the fundamental theorem of calculus: the integral of a rate of change gives the total change.

<Deep title="The formula, and what the symbols say">

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^{*}) \,\Delta x
$$

Here $$\Delta x = (b - a)/n$$ is the strip width and $$x_i^{*}$$ is a point inside strip $$i$$. The integral sign $$\int$$ is an elongated S for "sum", and $$dx$$ says the sum is over a thin width. The midpoint sum used in the widget chooses $$x_i^{*}$$ at each strip's middle.

</Deep>

## What to take forward

- An integral is the area under a curve, or a total of many small pieces.
- You can approximate it with thin strips, and the approximation improves as they multiply.
- It is the tool behind continuous probabilities and expected values.
- You rarely compute integrals by hand in ML; you need to know what they mean when a paper uses one.
