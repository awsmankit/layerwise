Many models have hidden variables: the topic behind a document, the cause behind an image, the true state behind a noisy sensor. Given the data, the question is what those hidden variables probably were. That answer is the **posterior**, and for most models it is too hard to compute exactly, because it needs sums or integrals over every possible hidden value. **Variational inference** avoids the calculation by choosing a simpler guess and making it as close to the truth as possible.

## Bound the thing you can't compute

Write $$p(x)$$ for the probability of the data. It needs a sum over hidden states, which is hard. The **evidence lower bound**, the ELBO, is a quantity you can compute for any guess $$q(z)$$:

$$
\text{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big]
$$

It is always less than or equal to $$\log p(x)$$. The gap between them is exactly the KL divergence from the guess $$q$$ to the true posterior $$p(z \mid x)$$:

$$
\log p(x) = \text{ELBO}(q) + D_{\text{KL}}\big(q(z) \parallel p(z \mid x)\big)
$$

So maximising the ELBO over $$q$$ does two things at once: it pushes up a lower bound on the evidence, and it pulls $$q$$ towards the true posterior.

## Try it

The hidden variable can be 0 or 1. The data is more likely under 0, so the true posterior puts 80 per cent on 0. Move the guess $$q(z = 0)$$. The ELBO is highest, and the gap is zero, when the guess matches 0.80. Anywhere else the ELBO sits below $$\log p(x)$$.

<Callout title="Why this powers modern generative models">

A variational autoencoder learns an encoder that gives a guess $$q$$ for each input and a decoder that explains the data. Training maximises the ELBO with gradients. The same idea sits behind many latent-variable models, which is why the KL divergence from the earlier lesson keeps reappearing: it is the price of the approximation.

</Callout>

## Where it came from

Variational bounds have long been used in statistical physics and in Bayesian statistics. In machine learning, variational methods for latent-variable models developed through the 1990s and 2000s, and Kingma and Welling's 2013 paper made the approach practical for deep networks trained with gradients.

<Deep title="Why the bound is never above the truth">

Start from $$\log p(x) = \log \sum_z p(x,z) = \log \sum_z q(z) \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}$$. Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm says the log of an average is at least the average of the logs, so $$\log p(x) \ge \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}$$. The right side is the ELBO. Equality holds exactly when $$q$$ equals the posterior.

</Deep>

## What to take forward

- The posterior over hidden variables is often too hard to compute exactly.
- Variational inference replaces it with a simpler guess and optimises that guess.
- The ELBO is a computable lower bound on the log evidence.
- The gap between the ELBO and the evidence is the KL divergence to the true posterior, so maximising the ELBO also improves the guess.
