Most matrices rotate and stretch the vectors they act on. An **eigenvector** is a special direction that the matrix only stretches, without turning. The stretch factor is its **eigenvalue**. Eigenvectors matter in ML because they point to the directions that carry the most information in a dataset.

## Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix keeps

If a matrix $$A$$ has an eigenvector $$v$$, then $$A v = \lambda v$$ for some number $$\lambda$$, the eigenvalue. Multiplying by $$A$$ just scales $$v$$. For most vectors, a matrix changes both the length and the direction. The eigenvectors are the exceptions, and they give the matrix's natural axes.

## Principal components: where the data spreads most

Take a cloud of points. Its **covariance matrix** records how the dials vary together. Its eigenvectors are the directions in which the cloud is stretched, and the largest eigenvalue belongs to the direction of greatest spread. That direction is the **first principal component**. Keeping only the top few components is principal component analysis (PCA), a way to shrink data while keeping most of its variation.

A dial that barely varies across the data tells you little about it, and removing it loses little. A direction along which the data spreads widely carries most of the differences between examples. ## Where it came from

Karl Pearson introduced principal components in 1901, to find the best-fitting line through a cloud of points. Harold Hotelling developed the method in the 1930s. Eigenvalues themselves came from studies of vibrations and quadratic forms in the 1700s and 1800s.

<Deep title="The angle formula, and why it works">

For points with variances $$S_{xx}$$, $$S_{yy}$$ and covariance $$S_{xy}$$, the spread along a direction at angle $$\theta$$ is $$\cos^2\theta \, S_{xx} + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta \, S_{xy} + \sin^2\theta \, S_{yy}$$. Setting its derivative to zero gives $$\tan 2\theta = \dfrac{2S_{xy}}{S_{xx} - S_{yy}}$$, which is the angle the widget reports. The largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix is the spread in that direction.

</Deep>

## Why principal components are eigenvectors

**Why are principal components eigenvectors, in any number of dimensions?** Let $$C$$ be the covariance matrix of the (centred) data and $$u$$ a unit direction, so $$|u| = 1$$. The variance of the data along $$u$$ is $$u^\top C u$$. We want the $$u$$ that maximises this while keeping $$|u| = 1$$. A Lagrange multiplier $$\lambda$$ handles the constraint: set the gradient of $$u^\top C u - \lambda (u^\top u - 1)$$ to zero, which gives

$$
C u = \lambda u
$$

So the best direction must be an eigenvector of $$C$$. Multiplying by $$u^\top$$ shows the variance along it is $$u^\top C u = \lambda$$, the eigenvalue. The top eigenvector is therefore the direction of greatest spread, the next is the greatest spread at right angles to it, and so on, in any number of dimensions. Each eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues is the share of the total variance that component keeps.

**Example.** Take $$C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$. Its eigenvalues are 3 and 1, with eigenvectors along $$(1, 1)$$ and $$(1, -1)$$. Along the unit vector $$(1,1)/\sqrt{2}$$ the variance is $$(2 + 1 + 1 + 2)/2 = 3$$, matching the eigenvalue. The first component keeps $$3/(3+1) = 75$$ per cent of the variance.

## What to take forward

- An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches; the stretch is its eigenvalue.
- Principal components are the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix.
- The first component is the direction of greatest spread.
- PCA keeps the top components to compress data while keeping most of its variation.
